### 1. Tautologies and Contradictions
These terms describe the "truth value" of a compound proposition regardless of the truth values of its individual components.

- **Tautology**: A compound proposition that is **always true**.
    - Example: $p \lor \neg p$ (A statement is either true or it is not).
- **Contradiction**: A compound proposition that is **always false**.
    - Example: $p \land \neg p$ (A statement cannot be both true and false at the same time).
- **Contingency**: A proposition that is neither a tautology nor a contradiction (it can be true or false depending on the inputs).

---

### 2. Logical Equivalence Laws
Two propositions $p$ and $q$ are **logically equivalent** ($p \equiv q$) if $p \leftrightarrow q$ is a tautology. Instead of drawing truth tables, we use these laws to simplify expressions:

| Law | Disjunction ($\lor$) | Conjunction ($\land$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Identity** | $p \lor F \equiv p$ | $p \land T \equiv p$ |
| **Domination** | $p \lor T \equiv T$ | $p \land F \equiv F$ |
| **Idempotent** | $p \lor p \equiv p$ | $p \land p \equiv p$ |
| **Double Negation** | $\neg(\neg p) \equiv p$ | |
| **Commutative** | $p \lor q \equiv q \lor p$ | $p \land q \equiv q \land p$ |
| **Associative** | $(p \lor q) \lor r \equiv p \lor (q \lor r)$ | $(p \land q) \land r \equiv p \land (q \land r)$ |
| **Distributive** | $p \lor (q \land r) \equiv (p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)$ | $p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)$ |
| **De Morgan's** | $\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q$ | $\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q$ |
| **Absorption** | $p \lor (p \land q) \equiv p$ | $p \land (p \lor q) \equiv p$ |
| **Negation** | $p \lor \neg p \equiv T$ | $p \land \neg p \equiv F$ |

**Important Implication Law:**
- $p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q$ (This is the most common way to remove an arrow in a proof).

---

### 3. How to Prove Logical Equivalences Using Laws
To prove that $A \equiv B$ without a truth table, you start with one side of the equivalence and apply the laws step-by-step until you reach the other side.

**Example: Prove that $\neg(p \lor (\neg p \land q)) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q$**

1.  $\neg(p \lor (\neg p \land q))$
2.  $\equiv \neg p \land \neg(\neg p \land q)$ (By **De Morgan's Law**)
3.  $\equiv \neg p \land (\neg(\neg p) \lor \neg q)$ (By **De Morgan's Law** again)
4.  $\equiv \neg p \land (p \lor \neg q)$ (By **Double Negation**)
5.  $\equiv (\neg p \land p) \lor (\neg p \land \neg q)$ (By **Distributive Law**)
6.  $\equiv F \lor (\neg p \land \neg q)$ (By **Negation Law**: $\neg p \land p$ is always false)
7.  $\equiv \neg p \land \neg q$ (By **Identity Law**)

**Tips for Proofs:**
- Always state which law you are using for each step.
- If you see an implication ($\to$), convert it to $\neg p \lor q$ first.
- Look for De Morgan's if there is a negation outside parentheses.
- Look for Distributive laws if you have a mix of $\land$ and $\lor$.

**ai**: In logic, rules of inference are usually written in a vertical stack (premises above a line, conclusion below). However, in "one line," they are expressed as **tautologies** in the form of a conditional statement:

$$(Premise_1 \land Premise_2 \land ...) \to Conclusion$$

Here are the standard rules of inference from your [[Midterm 1]] note written in one line:

| Rule Name | One-Line Logical Form |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Modus Ponens** | $[p \land (p \to q)] \to q$ |
| **Modus Tollens** | $[\neg q \land (p \to q)] \to \neg p$ |
| **Hypothetical Syllogism** | $[(p \to q) \land (q \to r)] \to (p \to r)$ |
| **Disjunctive Syllogism** | $[(p \lor q) \land \neg p] \to q$ |
| **Addition** | $p \to (p \lor q)$ |
| **Simplification** | $(p \land q) \to p$ |
| **Conjunction** | $[(p) \land (q)] \to (p \land q)$ |
| **Resolution** | $[(p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor r)] \to (q \lor r)$ |

### How to read these:
- The left side of the $\to$ (the antecedent) represents all your **given facts** or premises joined by "AND" ($\land$).
- The right side (the consequent) is the **conclusion** you are allowed to draw.
- Because these are rules of inference, the entire one-line statement is a **tautology** (it is always true).

### Example: Modus Ponens
- **Vertical form:**
  $p$
  $p \to q$
  $\therefore q$
- **One-line form:** $[p \land (p \to q)] \to q$
- **English:** "If $p$ is true, AND $p$ implies $q$, then $q$ must be true."

**ai**: Based on your [[Midterm 1]] note, here is a breakdown of the logical equivalence laws with explanations and examples for each.

### 1. Identity Laws
These laws state that a proposition's truth value is preserved when combined with a "neutral" element.
- **$p \land T \equiv p$**: "ANDing" something with True doesn't change it.
- **$p \lor F \equiv p$**: "ORing" something with False doesn't change it.
- **Example**: "It is raining AND True" is only true if it is actually raining.

### 2. Domination Laws
One value "dominates" the expression, regardless of what $p$ is.
- **$p \lor T \equiv T$**: If one part of an OR is True, the whole thing is True.
- **$p \land F \equiv F$**: If one part of an AND is False, the whole thing is False.
- **Example**: "I will win the lottery OR True." Since "True" is always true, the whole statement is true even if I lose the lottery.

### 3. Idempotent Laws
Repeating the same statement with the same operator doesn't change anything.
- **$p \lor p \equiv p$**
- **$p \land p \equiv p$**
- **Example**: "I am a student OR I am a student" is just a redundant way of saying "I am a student."

### 4. Double Negation Law
Two negatives cancel each other out.
- **$\neg(\neg p) \equiv p$**
- **Example**: "It is not the case that I am not happy" means "I am happy."

### 5. Commutative Laws
The order of the variables does not matter.
- **$p \lor q \equiv q \lor p$**
- **$p \land q \equiv q \land p$**
- **Example**: "I like tea AND I like coffee" is the same as "I like coffee AND I like tea."

### 6. Associative Laws
When using the **same** operator, the grouping (parentheses) does not matter.
- **$(p \lor q) \lor r \equiv p \lor (q \lor r)$**
- **$(p \land q) \land r \equiv p \land (q \land r)$**
- **Example**: (Apples and Bananas) and Cherries $\equiv$ Apples and (Bananas and Cherries).

### 7. Distributive Laws
This is like math: $a(b+c) = ab + ac$. You "distribute" the outer operator over the inner one.
- **$p \lor (q \land r) \equiv (p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)$**
- **$p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)$**
- **Example**: "I want soup AND (salad OR bread)" means "I want (soup AND salad) OR (soup AND bread)."

### 8. De Morgan's Laws
When you move a negation **inside** parentheses, the operator flips ($\land$ becomes $\lor$, and vice versa).
- **$\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q$**
- **$\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q$**
- **Example**: "It is NOT (sunny AND warm)" is the same as saying "It is NOT sunny OR it is NOT warm."

### 9. Absorption Laws
The "stronger" variable absorbs the "weaker" group.
- **$p \lor (p \land q) \equiv p$**
- **$p \land (p \lor q) \equiv p$**
- **Example**: "I am a student OR (I am a student AND I am rich)." If you are a student, the whole thing is true. If you aren't, the whole thing is false. The "rich" part becomes irrelevant.

### 10. Negation Laws
A statement combined with its exact opposite.
- **$p \lor \neg p \equiv T$** (A statement is either true or false—this is a Tautology).
- **$p \land \neg p \equiv F$** (A statement cannot be both true and false—this is a Contradiction).
- **Example**: "It is raining OR it is not raining" is always a true statement.

### 11. Implication Law (Very Important!)
This allows you to turn an "If...then" into an "OR" statement, which is necessary for most proofs.
- **$p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q$**
- **Example**: "If you pay me, I will work" is logically the same as "You didn't pay me OR I will work."

### 12. Biconditional Law
- **$p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \to q) \land (q \to p)$**
- **Example**: "I'll go if and only if you go" means "If I go then you go, AND if you go then I go."
