## From Chapter 4 Notes

What is the **Business Cycle**? #flashcard
Alternating periods of economic expansion and recession.

A period during which total production and total employment are increasing is called an {1:expansion}.

A period during which total production and total employment are decreasing is called a {1:recession}.

What is **Economic Growth**? #flashcard
The ability for an economy to produce increasing quantities of goods and services.

What is **GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**? #flashcard
The market value of all final goods & services produced in a region (country) in a given period of time (year).

In GDP calculation, we only include {1:current production} and not {2:used goods}.

A good or service that is purchased by its final user and is not included in the production of any other good or service is called a {1:final good or service}.

A good or service that is an input into another good or service is called an {1:intermediate good}.

Why is the value of intermediate goods not included in GDP? #flashcard
To avoid double counting; only the value of the final sale is used.

The circular flow of income and expenditure illustrates that the value of total production equals the value of total {1:income} and total {2:expenditure}.

What are **Transfer payments**? #flashcard
Payments by the government to households for which the government does not receive a new good or service in return. They are NOT included in GDP.

What are the three main methods of calculating GDP? #flashcard
1.  The Expenditure Approach
2.  The Income Approach
3.  The Value-Added Method
![[Pasted image 20250921141155.png]]

The formula for the Expenditure Approach to GDP is $Y = {1:C} + {2:I} + {3:G} + {4:Nx}$.

In the expenditure approach, what does 'C' stand for and what is its significance? #flashcard
Consumption; it is the largest component of GDP, representing spending by households on goods and services.

In the expenditure approach, what does 'I' stand for and what does it include? #flashcard
Investment; it includes Gross Fixed Capital Formation (purchases of fixed assets like buildings, machinery) and Investment in Inventories (changes in unsold products).

New housing is included in GDP as {1:investment} in the year it was built. Sales of existing homes are {2:not added}.

In the expenditure approach, what does 'G' stand for and what does it exclude? #flashcard
Government Spending; it includes final consumption spending and fixed capital formation by government, but excludes transfer payments.

In the expenditure approach, what does 'Nx' stand for and how is it calculated? #flashcard
Net Exports; it is calculated as Exports minus Imports.

What is **Statistical Discrepancy** in GDP calculation? #flashcard
One-half of the difference between the estimates of GDP generated by the expenditure approach and the income approach, arising from small errors in data and different estimation techniques.

The Income Approach to GDP sums up all the income received by Canadians as a result of production that takes place in Canada. This is also referred to as {1:Gross Domestic Income} or {2:Gross National Product}.

What is **Value Added** in the context of GDP calculation? #flashcard
The additional market value a firm gives a product, equal to the difference between the price the firm paid for intermediate goods and the price for which it sells the finished product.
![[Pasted image 20250921145248.png]]

List two shortcomings of GDP as a measure of total production. #flashcard
1.  Household Production (e.g., childcare, cleaning done by oneself)
2.  Informal Economy (transactions hidden from the government)

List three shortcomings of GDP as a measure of well-being. #flashcard
1.  Does not account for leisure time.
2.  Does not account for environmental quality.
3.  Does not show income distribution.
4.  Excludes non-market activities (e.g., volunteer work).
5.  Hard to capture product quality improvements.

What is **Nominal GDP**? #flashcard
Calculated by summing the current values of final goods and services, reflecting both price and quantity changes.

What is **Real GDP**? #flashcard
Calculated using prices from a designated base year to value goods and services in all other years, thus reflecting only changes in the quantity of goods and services produced.

Why is Real GDP a better measure of changes in production than Nominal GDP? #flashcard
Real GDP holds prices constant, separating price changes from quantity changes, giving a true picture of production growth.
![[Pasted image 20250921151754.png]]

What is the **GDP Deflator**? #flashcard
A measure of the price level, calculated as $\frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100$.

What is **Household Disposable Income**? #flashcard
Household Income minus Personal tax payments.
![[Pasted image 20250921153457.png]]

## From Chapter 5 Notes

What is the **Working-age Population**? #flashcard
Individuals aged 15 and older.

What is the **Labour Force**? #flashcard
All people who are working or actively looking for work (employed + unemployed).
![[Pasted image 20250928140220.png]]

People who don’t have a job but are willing and able to work and have looked for work in the last four weeks are considered {1:unemployed}.

People who are available for work but have not looked for a job during the previous four weeks because they believe no jobs are available for them are called {1:discouraged workers}. They are counted as {2:not in the labour force}.

The formula for the **Unemployment Rate** is $\frac{{\text{Number of unemployed}}}{{\text{Labour Force}}} \times 100\%$.

The formula for the **Labour Force Participation Rate** is $\frac{{\text{Labour Force}}}{{\text{Working-age population}}} \times 100\%$.

The formula for the **Employment-Population Ratio** is $\frac{{\text{Number of employed}}}{{\text{Working-age population}}} \times 100\%$.

What are the four types of unemployment? #flashcard
1.  Frictional unemployment
2.  Structural unemployment
3.  Cyclical unemployment
4.  Seasonal unemployment

**Frictional unemployment** happens when workers are {1:between jobs or searching for new ones}. It is {2:short-term} and unavoidable.

**Structural unemployment** happens when workers’ {1:skills don’t match the requirements of available jobs}. It often lasts {2:longer}.

**Cyclical unemployment** happens when a {1:recession} is occurring in the business cycle, leading firms to lay off workers.

**Seasonal unemployment** happens when jobs are only available at {1:certain times of the year}.

What is **Full Employment**? #flashcard
Occurs when the only remaining unemployment is structural and frictional unemployment.

What is the **Natural rate of unemployment**? #flashcard
The sum of frictional and structural unemployment. Fluctuations in the overall unemployment rate are mainly due to cyclical unemployment.

What is **Employment Insurance (EI)**? #flashcard
A government program that supports workers who lose their jobs, replacing part of their income to allow more time for job searching. It can increase the unemployment rate in the short term but leads to better job matches.
![[Pasted image 20251022185331.png]]

If the minimum wage is higher than the market-clearing wage, it can lead to {1:unemployment} because more people want to work than there are jobs available.
![[Pasted image 20251022185520.png]]

What are **Labour Unions**? #flashcard
Organizations of workers that bargain with employers for higher wages and better working conditions. In unionized industries, wages are often above market-clearing wages, leading to fewer workers.

What are **Efficiency Wages**? #flashcard
A higher-than-market wage that a firm pays to motivate workers to be more productive. This can lead to a quantity of labour supplied exceeding the quantity demanded, contributing to unemployment.

What is **Inflation**? #flashcard
A general increase in the price of goods and services over time (cost of living rises over time).

What is the **Inflation Rate**? #flashcard
The percentage increase in the price level from one year to the next.

What is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** used for? #flashcard
To measure changes in the prices faced by the average household.

The formula for **CPI** is {1:$\frac{{\text{cost in current year}}}{{\text{cost in base year}}} \times 100\%$}.

List three limitations of the CPI. #flashcard
1.  Substitution bias (assumes people buy the same items)
2.  Quality bias (price increases reflect improved quality)
3.  New product bias (basket not updated quickly enough)
4.  Outlet bias (doesn't reflect shifts to discount stores)

The **GDP Deflator** deals with goods {1:produced in Canada}, while the **CPI** deals with goods {2:bought by typical households} (which don't have to be Canadian).

What is the **Producer Price Index (PPI)**? #flashcard
An average of the prices received by producers of goods and services at all stages of production, including intermediate goods and raw materials.

The formula for **Real Wage** is $\frac{{\text{Nominal Wage}}}{{\text{CPI}}} \times 100$.
![[Pasted image 20250928171933.png]]

What is the **Nominal Interest Rate**? #flashcard
The stated interest rate on a loan.

What is the **Real Interest Rate**? #flashcard
The nominal interest rate corrected for the effect of inflation on the purchasing power of money.

The formula for **Real Interest Rate** is {1:Nominal Interest rate} - {2:Inflation rate}.

What are **Menu costs**? #flashcard
The costs to firms of changing prices.

If actual inflation is higher than expected, {1:borrowers} gain because they repay loans with money that has less value, while {2:lenders} lose.

## From Chapter 6 Notes

What is **Long-run economic growth**? #flashcard
The process by which rising productivity increases the average standard of living, best measured by increases in real GDP per capita over long periods.

The **Rule of 70** states that the number of years for a variable to double is approximately {1:$\frac{{70}}{{\text{Growth Rate}}}$}.

What is **Labour productivity ($Y/L$)**? #flashcard
The quantity of goods and services that can be produced by one worker or by one hour of work.

What is **Capital** in economics? #flashcard
The manufactured goods that are used to produce other goods and services (e.g., computers, factories, trucks).

What is **Human Capital**? #flashcard
The accumulated knowledge and skills workers acquire from education, training, or life experiences.

What is **Technological change**? #flashcard
An increase in the quantity of output firms can produce using a given quantity of inputs.

What is **Potential GDP**? #flashcard
The level of real GDP attained when all firms are producing at capacity.

What is the **Output Gap**? #flashcard
The percentage difference between actual GDP and potential GDP. A negative gap means the economy is not making full use of its resources.

What are **Retained Earnings**? #flashcard
Profits that are reinvested in the firm rather than paid to the firm's owners.

What is the **Financial System**? #flashcard
The system of financial markets and financial intermediaries through which firms acquire funds from households.

What are **Financial Markets**? #flashcard
Markets where financial securities, such as stocks and bonds, are bought and sold.

What are **Financial Intermediaries**? #flashcard
Go-betweens for borrowers and lenders, such as banks, mutual funds, pension funds, and insurance companies.

What are the three key services provided by the financial system for savers and borrowers? #flashcard
1.  Risk sharing
2.  Liquidity
3.  Information

What is **Private Saving**? #flashcard
What households retain from their income after purchasing goods and services (C) and paying taxes (T), plus transfers from the government (TR). ($S_{private} = Y + TR - C - T$)

What is **Public Saving**? #flashcard
The amount of tax revenue the government retains after paying for government purchases and making transfer payments to households. ($S_{public} = T - G - TR$)

In a closed economy, total saving (S) equals {1:investment (I)}. ($S = Y - C - G = I$)

A **Budget deficit** occurs when the government spends {1:more} than it collects in taxes, leading to {2:negative} public savings.

A **Budget surplus** occurs when the government spends {1:less} than it collects in taxes, leading to {2:positive} public savings and higher investment spending.

What is the **Market for loanable funds ($LF$)**? #flashcard
The interaction of borrowers and lenders that determines the market interest rate and the quantity of loanable funds exchanged.
![[Pasted image 20251023115107.png]]

The **Demand for Loanable Funds** is {1:downward sloping} because lower interest rates make more investment projects profitable.

The **Supply of Loanable Funds** is {1:upward sloping} because higher interest rates encourage more saving.

What is **Crowding Out**? #flashcard
A reduction in private investment caused by government budget deficits raising interest rates.

What is a **Business Cycle Peak**? #flashcard
The point when the expansion phase of the business cycle ends.

What is a **Business Cycle Trough**? #flashcard
The point when the recession phase of the business cycle ends.
![[Pasted image 20251005161000.png]]

A recession is typically defined as {1:two consecutive quarters} of negative real GDP growth.

During economic expansions, the inflation rate usually {1:increases}, and during recessions, it usually {2:decreases}.

Recessions cause the inflation rate to {1:fall}, but they cause the unemployment rate to {2:increase}.

## From Chapter 7 Notes

What is **Long-run economic growth**? #flashcard
The process by which rising productivity increases the average standard of living.

What is **Compounding** in economic growth? #flashcard
A process where small differences in interest rates (or growth rates) magnify over long periods of time, leading to large differences in living standards.

What is an **Economic growth model**? #flashcard
A model that explains growth rates in real GDP per capita over the long run, focusing on the causes of long-run increases in labour productivity.

What are the two key factors determining labour productivity? #flashcard
1.  Quantity of capital available to workers (physical and human capital)
2.  Level of technology (technological change)

What is **Physical capital**? #flashcard
Manufactured goods that are used to produce other goods and services (e.g., computers, factory buildings).

What is **Human capital**? #flashcard
The accumulated knowledge and skills that workers acquire from education and training or from their life experiences.

What is a **Just-in-time system**? #flashcard
A production system where goods are assembled from parts that arrive at the factory at exactly the time workers need them, improving efficiency.

What is a **Per-worker production function**? #flashcard
The relationship between real GDP per hour worked and capital per hour worked, holding the level of technology constant.
![[Pasted image 20251019145706.png]]

What is the **Law of diminishing returns**? #flashcard
States that as we add more of one input (e.g., capital) to a fixed quantity of another input (e.g., labour), output increases by smaller additional amounts.

Technological change shifts the per-worker production function {1:up}, allowing an economy to produce {2:more} real GDP per hour worked with the same quantity of capital per hour worked.
![[Pasted image 20251019145744.png]]

What is **New growth theory**? #flashcard
A model by Paul Romer arguing that individuals and firms respond to economic incentives affecting technological change, with knowledge capital being a key determinant of growth.

What is **Knowledge capital**? #flashcard
The accumulated knowledge, or stock of knowledge, which is a key determinant of economic growth and can lead to increasing returns at the economy-wide level.

A good is {1:non-rival} if one firm's use of it does not prevent other firms from using it (e.g., knowledge capital).

A good is {1:non-excludable} if, once known, it becomes widely available for everyone to use (e.g., knowledge capital).

What is a **Patent**? #flashcard
Gives a firm the exclusive legal right to a new product for a period of 20 years from the date the patent application is filed with the government.

What is **Creative destruction**? #flashcard
Joseph Schumpeter's view that new products unleash a “gale of creative destruction” that drives older products—and, often, the firms that produced them—out of the market.

What is a **General-purpose technology**? #flashcard
A technology that has the potential to affect many sectors of the economy (e.g., artificial intelligence).

What is the **Productivity J-curve**? #flashcard
A phenomenon where productivity initially declines when a general-purpose technology is introduced before rising significantly as businesses develop new processes to integrate it.

What is **Secular stagnation**? #flashcard
An extended period of slow growth, predicted by some economists due to insufficient demand for investment spending.

What is **Catch-up (or convergence)**? #flashcard
The prediction that poor countries will grow faster than rich countries, leading to convergence in levels of GDP per capita.

The ability of a government to enforce the laws of the country, particularly with respect to protecting private property and enforcing contracts.

What is **Corruption**? #flashcard
Government officials using their positions for personal gain, often undermining the rule of law and property rights.

What is the **Vicious cycle of poverty**? #flashcard
A situation where low incomes lead to low savings, which leads to low investment, slow economic growth, and thus continued low incomes and savings.

What is **Globalization**? #flashcard
The process of countries becoming more open to foreign trade and investment.

What is **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**? #flashcard
Occurs when a firm builds or purchases a facility in a foreign country.

What is **Foreign portfolio investment**? #flashcard
Occurs when an individual or a firm buys stocks or bonds issued in another country.

List three government policies that can promote long-run economic growth. #flashcard
1.  Enhancing property rights and the rule of law.
2.  Improving health and education.
3.  Promoting technological change (e.g., subsidizing R&D, patents).
4.  Promoting saving and investment (e.g., tax incentives).

What is **Brain drain**? #flashcard
Refers to highly educated and successful individuals leaving developing countries for high-income countries.

## From Chapter 8 Notes (Expanded)

What is the **Aggregate Expenditure (AE) Model**? #flashcard
A macroeconomic model that focuses on the short-run relationship between total spending and real GDP, assuming a constant price level.

What is the key idea of the AE Model? #flashcard
In any particular year, the level of real GDP is determined mainly by the level of aggregate expenditure.

What is the central assumption of the Aggregate Expenditure Model? #flashcard
The price level is constant. This means we don't have to worry about the difference between real and nominal GDP.

Planned Aggregate Expenditure ($AE$) is the total planned spending in an economy, represented by the formula $AE = {1:C} + {2:I} + {3:G} + {4:NX}$.

What does **Consumption ($C$)** represent in the AE model? #flashcard
Spending by households on goods and services.
![[University/Year 1/ECON 1BB3/Attachments/Pasted Graphic.png]]

What does **Planned Investment ($I$)** represent in the AE model? #flashcard
Planned spending by firms on capital goods (factories, machines) and by households on new homes.
![[Pasted Graphic 13.png]]

What does **Government Purchases ($G$)** represent in the AE model? #flashcard
Spending by all levels of government on goods and services (e.g., infrastructure, salaries).
![[Pasted Graphic 14.png]]

What does **Net Exports ($NX$)** represent in the AE model? #flashcard
Spending by foreign firms and households on Canadian goods and services minus spending by Canadian firms and households on foreign goods and services.
![[Pasted Graphic 15.png]]

The difference between planned and actual investment comes from unplanned changes in {1:inventories}.

What are **Inventories**? #flashcard
Products that have been produced but not yet sold.

If firms sell less than they expected, there is an unplanned {1:increase} in inventories, and actual investment will be {2:greater} than planned investment.

If firms sell more than they expected, there is an unplanned {1:decrease} in inventories, and actual investment will be {2:less} than planned investment.

When does macroeconomic equilibrium occur in the AE model? #flashcard
When total spending equals total production, i.e., Planned Aggregate Expenditure = GDP ($AE = Y$).

How does the economy adjust to macroeconomic equilibrium in the AE model? #flashcard
Through firms' responses to unplanned inventory changes, not through price adjustments (since the price level is assumed constant).

| If... | then... | and... |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Planned AE = GDP | Inventories don’t change | The economy is in macroeconomic equilibrium. |
| Planned AE < GDP | Inventories rise | GDP and employment decrease. |
| Planned AE > GDP | Inventories fall | GDP and employment increase. |

Consumption is the {1:largest} component of aggregate expenditure.

What are the five most important determinants of consumption? #flashcard
1.  **Current Disposable Income**: The most important determinant.
2.  **Household Wealth**: Assets minus liabilities.
3.  **Expected Future Income**: People prefer stable consumption.
4.  **The Price Level**: Affects the real value of household wealth.
5.  **The Interest Rate**: Affects the reward for saving and cost of borrowing.

What is the **Consumption Function**? #flashcard
The relationship between consumption spending and disposable income.

What is **Autonomous Consumption**? #flashcard
The level of consumption that occurs even when disposable income is zero, financed out of savings, and represents the intercept of the consumption function.

What is the **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)**? #flashcard
The slope of the consumption function; the amount by which consumption spending changes when disposable income changes.
$$\text{MPC} = \frac{\text{Change in Consumption}}{\text{Change in Disposable Income}} = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y_D}$$

The change in consumption can be calculated as: {1:Change in Disposable Income} $\times$ {2:MPC}.

The relationship between National Income and Disposable Income is: Disposable Income = {1:National Income} - {2:Net Taxes}.

For the economy as a whole, National Income ($Y$) = {1:Consumption ($C$)} + {2:Saving ($S$)} + {3:Taxes ($T$)}.

What is the **Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)**? #flashcard
The amount by which saving changes when disposable income changes.
$$\text{MPS} = \frac{\text{Change in Saving}}{\text{Change in Disposable Income}} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y_D}$$

The MPC and MPS must sum to {1:1}, because any additional dollar of income must be either consumed or saved (assuming taxes are constant).

What are the four most important determinants of planned investment? #flashcard
1.  **Expectations of Future Profits**: Firms invest when optimistic.
2.  **Interest Rate**: Higher rates make borrowing more expensive.
3.  **Taxes**: Corporate income taxes and investment tax incentives.
4.  **Cash Flow**: Higher profits lead to greater cash flow for investment.

Government Purchases ($G$) include spending by all levels of government on goods and services, but do not include {1:transfer payments}.

What are the three most important determinants of net exports? #flashcard
1.  **The Price Level in Canada vs. Other Countries**: Lower Canadian inflation increases NX.
2.  **The Growth Rate of GDP in Canada vs. Other Countries**: Faster Canadian GDP growth decreases NX.
3.  **The Exchange Rate**: A stronger Canadian dollar decreases NX.

In a 45°-line diagram (Keynesian cross), what does the **45° line** show? #flashcard
All points where planned aggregate expenditure equals real GDP ($AE = Y$).
![[Pasted Graphic 19.png]]

The **Aggregate Expenditure (AE) function** is an {1:upward-sloping} line showing the amount of planned AE at every level of GDP. Its slope is the {2:MPC}.

If AE is above the 45° line ($AE > Y$), inventories {1:fall}, and firms {2:increase} production, causing GDP to {3:rise} toward equilibrium.

If AE is below the 45° line ($AE < Y$), inventories {1:rise}, and firms {2:decrease} production, causing GDP to {3:fall} toward equilibrium.

When the AE line intersects the 45° line at a level of GDP below potential GDP, the economy is in {1:recession}.
![[Pasted Graphic 20.png]]

What is the **Multiplier Effect**? #flashcard
The process by which an initial change in autonomous expenditure leads to a larger, multiplied change in equilibrium real GDP.
![[Pasted Graphic 21.png]]

What is **Autonomous Expenditure**? #flashcard
Spending that does not depend on the level of GDP (e.g., planned investment, government purchases, net exports, and the autonomous part of consumption).

The formula for the **Multiplier** is $\frac{1}{{1 - {1:MPC}}}$.

The change in equilibrium real GDP due to a change in autonomous expenditure is: {1:Change in autonomous expenditure} $\times$ {2:Multiplier}.

List two key points about the Multiplier. #flashcard
1.  The effect occurs for both increases and decreases in autonomous expenditure.
2.  It makes the economy more sensitive to changes in autonomous spending.
3.  The larger the MPC, the larger the value of the multiplier.
4.  The simple formula overstates the true value because it ignores real-world factors like taxes, imports, and changes in the price level.

What is the **Paradox of Thrift**? #flashcard
In the short run, an attempt by many households to increase their saving can lead to a fall in aggregate expenditure and push the economy into a recession, potentially causing total saving to not actually increase.

What does the **Aggregate Demand (AD) curve** show? #flashcard
The relationship between the price level and the level of planned aggregate expenditure (or equilibrium real GDP).

A rising price level decreases consumption (wealth effect), investment (interest-rate effect), and net exports (international-trade effect). This shifts the AE curve {1:down}, leading to a {2:lower} equilibrium GDP, and thus a point {3:up and to the left} on the AD curve.

A falling price level increases consumption, investment, and net exports. This shifts the AE curve {1:up}, leading to a {2:higher} equilibrium GDP, and thus a point {3:down and to the right} on the AD curve.
