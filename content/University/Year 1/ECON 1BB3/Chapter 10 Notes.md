# What Is Money, and Why Do We Need It?

**LO 10.1** Define money and discuss the four functions of money.

The economic definition of **++[money](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-c95259de-1fd7-5981-8dae-f8f1dc4bd64f)++** is any asset that people are generally willing to accept in exchange for goods and services or for payment of debts. An **++[asset](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-05fac943-80a7-5241-b237-80e7aef62b19)++** is anything of value owned by a person or a firm.

## Barter and the Invention of Money

*   **Barter economies**: Economies in which goods and services are traded directly for other goods and services.
*   **Double coincidence of wants**: A major shortcoming of barter, where for a trade to occur, each person must want what the other has.
*   **++[Commodity money](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-d8101684-231a-547c-b608-2f28b665b9b7)++**: A good used as money that also has value independent of its use as money (e.g., beaver pelts).
*   Money makes exchange easier, allowing people to specialize, become more productive, and earn higher incomes.

## The Functions of Money

Anything used as money must serve four key functions:

1.  **Medium of Exchange**: Sellers are willing to accept it for goods or services.
2.  **Unit of Account**: A way of measuring value in the economy in terms of money, giving each good a single price.
3.  **Store of Value**: Allows people to easily store value for future use.
    *   **Liquidity**: The ease with which an asset can be converted into the medium of exchange. Money is the most liquid asset.
4.  **Standard of Deferred Payment**: Facilitates exchange over time by providing a way to express debt payments.
    *   **Purchasing power**: The value of money, its ability to buy goods and services. Inflation causes a decline in purchasing power.

## What Can Serve as Money?

Five criteria make an asset suitable for use as a medium of exchange:

1.  The asset must be *acceptable* to most people.
2.  It should be of *standardized quality*.
3.  It should be *durable*.
4.  It should be *valuable* relative to its weight.
5.  It should be *divisible*.

### Commodity Money

*   Has value independent of its use as money (e.g., gold).
*   Problem: The money supply is difficult for the government to control.

### Fiat Money

*   **++[Fiat money](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-b131e4e8-6376-5cee-ac1c-392c06fa9df5)++**: Money that has no value except as money.
*   Issued by a **central bank**, an agency of the government that regulates the money supply. The **++[Bank of Canada](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-175c6fff-bdba-541d-9da4-d692d4db48ad)++** is Canada's central bank.
*   Canadian dollars are *legal tender*, meaning the federal government requires them to be accepted in payment of debts and taxes.
*   Acceptance relies on households' and firms' confidence that the dollars will not lose much value.

# How Is Money Measured in Canada Today?

**LO 10.2** Discuss the definitions of the money supply used in Canada today.

The Bank of Canada uses six different definitions of the money supply: M1+, M1++, M2, M2+, M2++, and M3.

## The M1+ and M1++ Definitions of the Money Supply

**Figure 10.1 Components of the Money Supply**
![[742EC7C6-8ACB-45E8-B8F6-2CAB1FEE7D3D.png]]

*   **++[M1+](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-5bba27f5-ceeb-583e-bba9-62b7e6a80bae)++**: The narrowest definition, includes:
    1.  *Currency*: Paper money and coins held by households and firms (not by banks).
    2.  Value of all chequable deposits at chartered banks, trust and mortgage loan companies (TMLs), and credit unions and *caisses populaires* (CUCPs).
*   **++[M1++](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-58d54990-e120-52d4-aa3a-93eb194c3753)++**: Includes everything in M1+ plus all non-chequable deposits at chartered banks, TMLs, and CUCPs.

## The M2, M2+, and M2++ Definitions of the Money Supply

*   **++[M2](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-32d332da-761f-54df-b959-e5887b6b94cb)++**: Includes currency, personal deposits at chartered banks, non-personal demand and notice deposits at chartered banks, and fixed-term deposits.
*   **++[M2+](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-7e8d3130-95a2-5144-9086-30ca68521ad6)++**: Includes everything in M2 plus deposits at TMLs, deposits at CUCPs, life insurance company individual annuities, personal deposits at government-owned savings institutions, and money market mutual funds.
*   **++[M2++](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-85e9f608-8229-5c83-9032-d716d4f6799a)++**: The broadest definition, includes everything in M2+ plus other retail instruments and non-money market mutual funds.

## The M3 Definition of the Money Supply

*   **++[M3](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-862a51f8-b629-5a4b-8729-a7c5c929bfd6)++**: Includes everything in M2 plus non-personal term deposits at chartered banks and foreign currency deposits of residents at chartered banks.

**Key Points about the Money Supply**:
1.  The money supply consists of *both* currency and chequing and non-chequing account deposits.
2.  Banks play an important role in how the money supply increases and decreases.

## Don’t Confuse Money with Income or Wealth

> [!NOTE] DON’T CONFUSE MONEY WITH INCOME OR WEALTH
> *   Your **wealth** is the value of your assets minus your debts.
> *   Your **income** is your earnings during the year.
> *   Your **money** is just equal to what you have in currency and chequing accounts.
>
> Economists use *money* to refer to currency plus chequing account deposits, which is distinct from wealth or income.

## Solved Problem 10.1
### The Definitions of M1+ and M2++

**Problem**: Suppose you decide to withdraw \$2000 from your chequing account and use the money to buy a mutual fund. Briefly explain how this will affect M1+ and M2++.

**Solution**:
*   **M1+**: Funds in chequable deposit accounts are included in M1+. Withdrawing \$2000 from your chequing account reduces M1+ by \$2000.
*   **M2++**: Mutual funds are included in M2++. The \$2000 was already counted in M1+ (and thus M2++). Moving it from a chequing account to a mutual fund means it remains within the broader definition of M2++. Therefore, M2++ remains unchanged.

## What about Credit Cards and Debit Cards?

*   Credit cards are not money; they are a form of short-term loan from the issuing bank.
*   Debit cards are not money; they are a way to access funds directly from your chequing account (which is part of the money supply).

# How Do Banks Create Money?

**LO 10.3** Explain how banks create money.

Canada has a **++[fractional reserve banking system](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-dc3e6d96-ab4d-547a-8e20-41ed0a942542)++**, meaning banks keep less than 100% of deposits as reserves. Banks accept deposits and make loans, playing a key role in the money supply process.

*   **Assets**:
    *   **++[Reserves](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-05227046-7431-531d-a109-ff214b0be04a)++**: Deposits a bank has retained. Held as *vault cash* or on deposit with the Bank of Canada.
        *   **++[Desired reserves](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-132df694-c6f9-5d60-8614-af65124c13f3)++**: The fraction of deposits banks desire to keep as reserves.
        *   **++[Desired reserve ratio](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-338bcdcc-ed20-5fa6-8a8a-8ea26a174392)++ ($RR$)**: The minimum fraction of deposits banks desire to keep as reserves.
        *   **++[Excess reserves](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-05b6cfcc-ec7a-5169-ac4d-f3ec86d257db)++**: Reserves held over and above the desired amounts.
    *   Loans: Typically the largest asset.
    *   Securities: Holdings of government bonds.
*   **Liabilities**:
    *   Deposits: Largest liability (chequing, savings, certificates of deposit).
    *   Borrowings: From other banks or the Bank of Canada.

## Using T-accounts to Show How a Bank Can Create Money

A T-account shows how a transaction *changes* a bank’s balance sheet. Assume banks voluntarily maintain reserves equal to 10% of their chequing account deposits.

1.  **Initial Deposit**: You deposit \$1000 in currency into Bank of Montreal.
    *   Bank of Montreal's T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$1000 | Deposits + \$1000 |
    *   Initially, the money supply does not change (currency decreases by \$1000, chequing deposit increases by \$1000).

2.  **Bank Makes a Loan**: Bank of Montreal keeps \$100 (10% of \$1000) as reserves and loans out \$900 by increasing the borrower's chequing account.
    *   Bank of Montreal's T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$1000 | Deposits + \$1000 |
        | Loans + \$900 | Deposits + \$900 |
    *   The money supply increases by \$900 (the new chequing deposit).

3.  **Cheque Clears**: The borrower spends the \$900, and the seller deposits it in Royal Bank. Royal Bank sends the cheque to Bank of Montreal.
    *   Bank of Montreal's T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves - \$900 | Deposits - \$900 |
    *   Royal Bank's T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$900 | Deposits + \$900 |

4.  **Royal Bank Makes a Loan**: Royal Bank keeps \$90 (10% of \$900) as reserves and loans out \$810.
    *   Royal Bank's T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$90 | Deposits + \$900 |
        | Loans + \$810 | |
    *   The money supply increases by another \$810.

This process continues, with each bank lending out 90% of new deposits, creating new money.

## The Simple Deposit Multiplier

*   **++[Simple deposit multiplier](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-c1db8f6d-1d4c-5d1e-a6b3-8b2021e9bc8a)++**: The ratio of the amount of deposits created by banks to the amount of new reserves.
*   Formula:
    $$ \text{Simple Deposit Multiplier} = \frac{1}{\text{Desired Reserve Ratio}} = \frac{1}{RR} $$
*   Total increase in chequing account deposits from an increase in bank reserves:
    $$ \text{Total change in chequing account deposits} = \text{Change in bank reserves} \times \frac{1}{RR} $$
    For example, if \$100,000 in currency is deposited and $RR = 0.10$:
    $$ \text{Total change in chequing account deposits} = \$100,000 \times \frac{1}{0.10} = \$1,000,000 $$

## Don’t Confuse Assets and Liabilities

> [!NOTE] DON’T CONFUSE ASSETS AND LIABILITIES
> *   A chequing account deposit is a **liability** to a bank (money owed to the account owner) but an **asset** to the account owner.
> *   A car loan is a **liability** to you (a debt you owe) but an **asset** to the bank (money owed to the bank).

## Solved Problem 10.2
### Showing How Banks Create Money

**Problem**: Deposit \$5000 in currency into Royal Bank. Assume $RR = 0.10$.

*   **a. Initial effect on Royal Bank's balance sheet.**
    *   Royal Bank T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$5000 | Deposits + \$5000 |

*   **b. Royal Bank makes maximum loan.**
    *   Royal Bank keeps \$500 (10% of \$5000) and loans out \$4500.
    *   Royal Bank T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$5000 | Deposits + \$5000 |
        | Loans + \$4500 | Deposits + \$4500 |

*   **c. Loan cheque clears at Bank of Montreal.**
    *   Royal Bank T-account (after cheque clears):
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves - \$4500 | Deposits - \$4500 |
    *   Bank of Montreal T-account:
        | Assets | Liabilities |
        | :----- | :---------- |
        | Reserves + \$4500 | Deposits + \$4500 |

*   **d. Maximum increase in chequing account deposits and money supply.**
    *   Maximum increase in chequing account deposits:
        $$ \$5000 \times \frac{1}{0.10} = \$50,000 $$
    *   Maximum increase in money supply: The initial \$5000 in currency is no longer counted once deposited.
        $$ \text{Increase in money supply} = \$50,000 - \$5000 = \$45,000 $$

## The Simple Deposit Multiplier versus the Real-World Deposit Multiplier

The simple deposit multiplier relies on two key assumptions that reduce its real-world value:
1.  *Banks always want to hold the same ratio of reserves to deposits.* In reality, banks may hold more excess reserves, especially if the central bank pays interest on reserves or due to tighter regulations. More reserves held means a smaller deposit multiplier.
2.  *The whole amount of every cheque is deposited in a bank; no one takes any of it out as currency.* In reality, households and firms hold some currency, which reduces the amount of reserves entering the banking system and thus the multiplier effect.

**Important Conclusions**:
1.  When banks gain reserves, they make new loans, and the money supply expands.
2.  When banks lose reserves, they reduce their loans, and the money supply contracts.

# The Bank of Canada

**LO 10.4** Describe how the Bank of Canada manages the money supply.

Canada operates a **++[fractional reserve banking system](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-dc3e6d96-ab4d-547a-8e20-41ed0a942542)++**.
*   **++[Bank run](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-cf0b682d-82d5-52db-b195-10f43947276d)++**: When many depositors simultaneously withdraw money from a bank.
*   **++[Bank panic](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-0b06537f-2d10-5eab-930a-ccafbaa652cd)++**: When many banks experience runs at the same time.
*   A central bank acts as a *lender of last resort* to help stop bank panics by making loans to banks.

## The Establishment of the Bank of Canada

*   Created in 1934.
*   Governed by a **++[board of directors](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-01859a53-8a95-5225-b6df-66745f6e601d)++** (15 members).
*   **++[Governing council](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-b50a1c17-653c-51c1-9a5c-208ac87e4afc)++**: Chaired by the governor, collectively responsible for the *Monetary Policy Report*.
*   **++[Monetary policy](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-f658eba0-194e-5c91-bb32-e2bf148f2495)++** objectives are determined jointly by the Bank of Canada and the Minister of Finance.

## The Bank of Canada’s Operating Band for the Overnight Interest Rate

The Bank of Canada signals its monetary policy stance by announcing a target for the **++[overnight interest rate](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-d0c474ec-91da-56fd-85be-855dec8395fe)++**, known as the **++[key policy rate](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-87865f41-2cc7-5f72-82f6-f22b522ac367)++**. This rate refers to **++[collateralized transactions](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-f224497b-36d2-540c-8f2b-42cccc4ffa70)++** in the overnight interbank market.

**Figure 10.3 Operating Band for the Overnight Interest Rate**
![[B496F37D-0070-454F-974F-835C26A1A24B.png]]
*The upper limit of the operating band for the overnight interest rate defines the bank rate (or lending rate), and the lower limit of the operating band defines the deposit rate.*

*   **++[Operating band](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-b774a620-53ad-57a3-a23d-77883b57d832)++**: A channel of 50 basis points (0.5%) for the overnight interest rate.
    *   **++[Bank rate](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-0c213bd4-e137-5aeb-85d2-a74631c07a84)++ ($i_B$)**: The upper limit of the operating band. The interest rate the Bank of Canada charges financial institutions for overdraft loans to cover negative **++[settlement balances](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-66e3d144-06f9-5c21-9db8-e902b6e70d30)++**. These loans are called **++[advances to banks](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-be4dd1a1-f09d-5ef9-8956-c6a5470d74e9)++**.
    *   The lower limit is the rate the Bank of Canada pays on deposits to financial institutions ($i_B - 50$ basis points).
    *   The midpoint is the target for the overnight interest rate.
*   The Bank of Canada uses **++[standing liquidity facilities](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-1107f870-6b87-5ce8-b014-640f4dfc3bc8)++** to implement the operating band.

## How the Bank of Canada Implements Monetary Policy

The Bank of Canada uses two main tools:

1.  **++[Open market buyback operations](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-0e1663be-0345-54b1-b964-0ab1e729c4e9)++**: The buying and selling of government securities.
    *   **++[Purchase and Resale Agreements (PRAs)](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-edf77426-553d-5409-8879-704499d01071)++**: Bank of Canada buys securities, increasing bank settlement balances, lowering short-term interest rates, and expanding the money supply. Used to reduce upward pressure on the overnight rate.
    *   **++[Sale and Repurchase Agreements (SRAs)](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-a3f0e4b3-9729-580f-ae4b-ae7a8ac39b8e)++**: Bank of Canada sells securities, shrinking bank reserves and the **++[monetary base](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-c29fa6f7-3516-5490-844f-2c06791f7922)++**, raising short-term interest rates, and contracting the money supply. Used to reduce downward pressure on the overnight rate.
    *   These operations are the primary determinants of changes in interest rates and the money supply.

2.  **Lending to financial institutions**:
    *   Banks can borrow from the Bank of Canada at the bank rate ($i_B$), increasing their reserves and expanding the money supply.
    *   The Bank of Canada acts as the lender of last resort to prevent financial panics.

## The Bank of Canada’s Approach to Monetary Policy

*   Goal: Keep the inflation rate within a target range of 1% to 3%, with a 2% midpoint.
*   It changes the policy rate to influence other short-term interest rates, the exchange rate, and economic activity.

*   **To prevent inflation from falling below the target range (easing monetary conditions)**:
    *   Lowers the target and operating band for the overnight interest rate.
    *   Reduces interest rates and the value of the dollar.
    *   Increases money supply, aggregate demand, and the price level.

    **Figure 10.4 How the Bank of Canada Keeps the Rate of Inflation from Falling Below the Target Range**
    ![[96763B2D-58BE-4CBA-BEA0-44DADA3FE41C.png]]

*   **To prevent inflation from moving above the target range (tightening monetary conditions)**:
    *   Raises the target and operating band for the overnight interest rate.
    *   Increases interest rates and the value of the dollar.
    *   Decreases money supply, aggregate demand, and the inflation rate.

## The “Shadow Banking System” and the Financial Crisis of 2007–2009

*   **Commercial banks**: Accept deposits and lend funds.
*   Two important developments: (1) Banks began to resell loans, and (2) nonbank financial firms became important sources of credit.

*   **++[Security](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-8eec7bc4-6180-5e0b-8a28-783d0bec1a3a)++**: A financial asset that can be bought and sold in a financial market.
*   **Primary market**: Where a financial asset is first sold.
*   **Secondary market**: Where an investor resells an asset.
*   **++[Securitization](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-9a754f0a-4844-5f2c-9bc2-f3f8aafe38a6)++**: The process of bundling loans (e.g., residential mortgages) into securities that can be bought and sold in financial markets (e.g., *mortgage-backed securities*).

### The Shadow Banking System

*   Refers to investment banks, money market mutual funds, hedge funds, and other non-bank financial firms.
*   These firms perform functions similar to commercial banks but are generally:
    1.  Not regulated by government agencies.
    2.  More highly *leveraged* (rely more on borrowed money), increasing potential profits and losses.

### The Financial Crisis Of 2007–2009

*   Caused by problems in the US housing market, leading to defaults on mortgages and losses in mortgage-backed securities.
*   Highly leveraged firms in the shadow banking system suffered large losses.
*   A *credit crunch* resulted as banks and financial firms sold assets and cut back on lending, worsening the recession.

# The Quantity Theory of Money

**LO 10.5** Explain the quantity theory of money and use it to explain how high rates of inflation occur.

## Connecting Money and Prices: The Quantity Equation

*   **Quantity equation**:
    $$ M \times V = P \times Y $$
    *   $M$: Money supply
    *   $V$: **++[Velocity of money](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-ddbb9551-d11b-5a30-bd9d-b01c0ebc5a92)++**: The average number of times each dollar of the money supply is used to purchase goods and services included in GDP.
    *   $P$: Price level
    *   $Y$: Real output (real GDP)
*   The quantity equation is an identity; it must always hold true.
*   **++[Quantity theory of money](https://plus.pearson.com/epub/bronte/BRNT-SITHABGCXI/v12/OPS/xhtml/glossary.xhtml#key-55335b35-c536-566b-9424-b2c0b78fbd32)++**: Irving Fisher's theory that velocity ($V$) is constant.

## The Quantity Theory Explanation of Inflation

Using growth rates, the quantity equation becomes:
$$ \text{Growth rate of } M + \text{Growth rate of } V = \text{Growth rate of } P + \text{Growth rate of } Y $$
Since the growth rate of the price level is the inflation rate, and assuming $V$ is constant (so its growth rate is zero):
$$ \text{Growth rate of } M = \text{Inflation rate} + \text{Growth rate of } Y $$
This equation predicts:
1.  If the money supply grows faster than real GDP, there will be inflation.
2.  If the money supply grows slower than real GDP, there will be deflation.
3.  If the money supply grows at the same rate as real GDP, the price level will be stable.

*   In the long run, inflation results from the money supply growing at a faster rate than real GDP.

## How Accurate Are Forecasts of Inflation Based on the Quantity Theory?

*   Velocity can fluctuate in the short run, so the quantity theory may not provide good short-run forecasts.
*   Over the long run, there is a strong link between changes in the money supply and inflation.

## High Rates of Inflation

*   **Hyperinflation**: A rate of inflation that exceeds 50% per month.
*   Caused by central banks increasing the money supply at a rate far in excess of real GDP growth.
*   High inflation causes money to lose its value rapidly, leading to severe economic problems.
*   Governments often cause high inflation by expanding the money supply rapidly to finance spending when they cannot raise enough through taxes or by selling bonds to the public.
