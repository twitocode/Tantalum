# What Is Monetary Policy?
**Monetary policy**: The actions the Bank of Canada takes to manage the money supply and interest rates to achieve its macroeconomic policy objectives.

## The Goals of Monetary Policy
The Bank of Canada has four main monetary policy goals:
1.  Price stability
2.  High employment
3.  Stability of financial markets and institutions
4.  Economic growth

### Price Stability
-   Rising prices reduce the usefulness of money as a medium of exchange and a store of value.
-   Policymakers aim for price stability, especially after the dramatic inflation of the 1970s.
-   The Bank of Canada aims to keep inflation between 1 and 3 percent.
-   Low inflation over the long run allows the price system to efficiently allocate resources and gives the Bank flexibility to increase aggregate demand to fight recessions.

![[AA9E09F4-A8DD-4FDC-B8A7-4B9CD877C1C9.png]]

### High Employment
-   Unemployed workers and underused factories reduce GDP below its potential level.
-   Unemployment causes financial distress and lower self-esteem for workers.
-   This goal extends beyond the Bank of Canada to other federal government branches.

### Stability of Financial Markets and Institutions
-   Firms need to borrow funds for production; savers look for financial investments.
-   The Bank of Canada promotes stability to ensure an efficient flow of funds from savers to borrowers.
-   The 2007–2009 and 2020 financial crises highlighted the importance of this goal.
-   **Shadow banking system**: Investment banks, money market mutual funds, and other financial firms that can face liquidity problems by borrowing short-term and investing long-term.
    -   These firms can experience crises if investors stop providing short-term loans.
    -   The Bank of Canada intervened in 2008 and 2020 to ease liquidity problems and prevent increased recession severity.

### Economic Growth
-   Economic growth is crucial for raising living standards.
-   Policymakers aim for *stable* economic growth to allow accurate planning and encourage investment.
-   Government policy (e.g., tax laws) may be better suited to increasing saving and investment than the Bank of Canada.
-   Some economists question the Bank of Canada's role in promoting economic growth beyond achieving price stability, high employment, and financial stability.

> [!NOTE]
> While the Bank of Canada has multiple goals, price stability has historically been the most important. However, financial market stability gained new emphasis during the 2007–2009 and 2020 crises.

# The Money Market and the Bank of Canada’s Choice of Monetary Policy Targets
The Bank of Canada uses policy tools (open market buyback operations, lending to financial institutions) to achieve its goals. Sometimes, goals conflict (e.g., raising interest rates to fight inflation may increase unemployment).

## Monetary Policy Targets
-   The Bank of Canada cannot directly affect inflation or employment.
-   It uses **monetary policy targets**: variables it can affect directly, which in turn influence real GDP, employment, and the price level.
-   The two main targets are the money supply and the interest rate. The Bank of Canada typically uses the interest rate as its policy target.
-   During the 2007–2009 global financial crisis, new policy tools were developed due to market disruptions.

## The Demand for Money
-   The **money market** brings together the demand and supply for money.
-   The demand curve for money is downward sloping.
    -   **Money**: Currency in circulation plus chequing account deposits.
    -   Households and firms choose between holding money (liquid, but low/zero interest) and other financial assets (pay interest, but less liquid).
    -   **Opportunity cost**: The interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money.
    -   When interest rates on financial assets (e.g., Treasury bills) are low, the opportunity cost of holding money is low, so the quantity of money demanded is high.
    -   When interest rates are high, the opportunity cost of holding money is high, so the quantity of money demanded is low.

![[ADD2B3BE-4C8F-467A-AF18-1B8AB3974286.png]]

## Shifts in the Money Demand Curve
The money demand curve is drawn holding constant all variables other than the interest rate. Changes in these other variables cause the curve to shift.
The three most important variables that shift the money demand curve are:
1.  **Changes in real GDP**:
    -   Increase in real GDP $\rightarrow$ more buying/selling $\rightarrow$ increased demand for money as a medium of exchange $\rightarrow$ money demand curve shifts right.
    -   Decrease in real GDP $\rightarrow$ decreased demand for money $\rightarrow$ money demand curve shifts left.
2.  **Changes in the price level**:
    -   Higher price level $\rightarrow$ more money needed for transactions $\rightarrow$ money demand curve shifts right.
    -   Lower price level $\rightarrow$ less money needed for transactions $\rightarrow$ money demand curve shifts left.
3.  **Technology**:
    -   More widespread use of mobile wallets/cashless payments $\rightarrow$ decreased demand for money at each interest rate $\rightarrow$ money demand curve shifts left.

![[A0E731B7-5F62-49DE-A925-25BEB21E610A.jpg]]

## How the Bank of Canada Manages the Money Supply: A Quick Review
-   To **increase** the money supply: The Bank of Canada purchases government of Canada securities. Sellers deposit funds, increasing bank reserves. Banks loan out reserves, creating new chequing account deposits and expanding the money supply.
-   To **decrease** the money supply: The Bank of Canada sells Canada securities, decreasing bank reserves and contracting the money supply.

## Equilibrium in the Money Market
-   Equilibrium occurs where the money demand curve crosses the money supply curve.
-   Assuming the Bank of Canada controls the money supply, the money supply curve is a vertical line (changes in interest rate have no effect on quantity supplied).

![[3B396FC2-F919-42C5-9DB5-EB1B2C00381A.avif]]

-   **Increase in money supply**: Shifts the money supply curve to the right, causing the equilibrium interest rate to fall.
    -   Households/firms initially hold more money than desired. They use this excess money to buy short-term financial assets or deposit it in interest-paying accounts.
    -   Increased demand for these assets/deposits allows banks and sellers to offer lower interest rates.
    -   Lower interest rates reduce the opportunity cost of holding money, leading to a movement down the money demand curve until equilibrium is restored.
-   **Decrease in money supply**: Shifts the money supply curve to the left, causing the equilibrium interest rate to rise.
    -   Households/firms initially hold less money than desired. They sell short-term financial assets or withdraw funds from interest-paying accounts to increase money holdings.
    -   Banks must offer higher interest rates to retain depositors; sellers of securities must offer higher rates to find buyers.
    -   Rising short-term interest rates increase the opportunity cost of holding money, leading to a movement up the money demand curve until equilibrium is restored.

![[7C4103D1-2D70-4BA9-87A4-F980DC2F7D96.avif]]

## A Tale of Two Interest Rates
1.  **Loanable funds model**: Concerned with the *long-term real rate of interest*. Relevant for savers purchasing long-term securities, firms financing long-term investment projects, and households taking out mortgage loans.
2.  **Money market model**: Concerned with the *short-term nominal rate of interest*. Most affected by Bank of Canada policy.

-   Often, movements in the short-term nominal interest rate are connected to movements in the long-term real interest rate, though sometimes with a delay.

## Choosing a Monetary Policy Target
-   The Bank of Canada can target either the money supply or the interest rate.
-   It has generally focused more on the interest rate, specifically the **overnight interest rate**.

## The Importance of the Overnight Interest Rate
-   Banks keep a fraction of deposits as reserves (currency or deposits with the Bank of Canada).
-   Banks typically invest excess reserves. When they need additional reserves, they borrow in the **overnight funds market** from other banks.
-   **Overnight interest rate**: The interest rate banks charge one another on loans in the overnight funds market (usually very short term).
-   The Bank of Canada announces a **target for the overnight interest rate** on eight fixed dates annually.
-   It sets an **operating band** (50 basis points wide, sometimes 25 basis points during crises) for the overnight interest rate. The actual rate is determined by the supply and demand for reserves.
-   Through open market buyback operations, the Bank can influence the supply of reserves to keep the overnight interest rate close to its target.

![[F91573A9-2ED9-4D70-8C4D-02AFA9793788.avif]]

-   Changes in the overnight interest rate usually lead to changes in other short-term and long-term interest rates (e.g., Canada bonds, corporate bonds, mortgages), though the effect on long-term rates may be lagged.

# Monetary Policy and Economic Activity
The Bank of Canada uses the overnight interest rate as a target because it can control it, and changes in it can affect real GDP, employment, and prices.
-   The Bank primarily affects economic variables by influencing *long-term real interest rates*.
-   We assume the Bank can use changes in the overnight interest rate to affect long-term real interest rates.

## How Interest Rates Affect Aggregate Demand
Changes in interest rates affect **aggregate demand** (total spending in the economy), specifically consumption, investment, and net exports. Government purchases are generally not affected.

-   **Consumption**:
    -   Lower interest rates on loans $\rightarrow$ increased household spending on consumer durables (e.g., cars, furniture) by reducing monthly payments.
    -   Higher interest rates $\rightarrow$ reduced spending on consumer durables.
    -   Higher interest rates $\rightarrow$ increased return to saving $\rightarrow$ households save more, spend less.
    -   Lower interest rates $\rightarrow$ reduced return to saving $\rightarrow$ households save less, spend more.
-   **Investment**:
    -   Firms borrow (corporate bonds, bank loans) to finance spending on machinery, equipment, R&D, buildings.
    -   Higher interest rates $\rightarrow$ more expensive to borrow $\rightarrow$ fewer investment projects.
    -   Lower interest rates $\rightarrow$ less expensive to borrow $\rightarrow$ more investment projects.
    -   Lower interest rates can also increase stock prices, making it easier for firms to raise funds for investment.
    -   Household spending on new homes is also investment. Lower mortgage rates $\rightarrow$ more new homes purchased.
-   **Net Exports**:
    -   Net exports = foreign spending on Canadian goods/services - Canadian spending on foreign goods/services.
    -   Depends on the exchange rate.
    -   If Canadian interest rates rise relative to other countries $\rightarrow$ investing in Canadian financial assets becomes more desirable $\rightarrow$ increased demand for Canadian dollars $\rightarrow$ value of the dollar rises $\rightarrow$ Canadian exports fall, imports rise $\rightarrow$ net exports fall.
    -   If Canadian interest rates decline relative to other countries $\rightarrow$ value of the Canadian dollar falls $\rightarrow$ net exports rise.

## The Effects of Monetary Policy on Real GDP and the Price Level
Using the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model (assuming no economic growth, so LRAS is fixed):

-   **Expansionary Monetary Policy**:
    -   Used when the economy is in a recession (real GDP below potential GDP, high unemployment).
    -   Bank of Canada **increases the money supply** and **decreases interest rates**.
    -   Lower interest rates $\rightarrow$ increase in consumption, investment, and net exports $\rightarrow$ aggregate demand curve shifts right.
    -   Real GDP increases, price level rises.
    -   Goal: Return real GDP to its potential level and achieve high employment.

![[739BED52-B0A9-4DCB-A0DF-20A434FA69D6.jpg]]

-   **Contractionary Monetary Policy**:
    -   Used when real GDP is above potential GDP (firms producing beyond normal capacity, very low unemployment, rising wages/prices).
    -   Bank of Canada **decreases the money supply** and **increases interest rates**.
    -   Higher interest rates $\rightarrow$ decrease in consumption, investment, and net exports $\rightarrow$ aggregate demand curve shifts left.
    -   Real GDP decreases, price level falls.
    -   Goal: Reduce upward pressure on wages and prices, returning real GDP to its potential level to achieve price stability.

## Can the Bank of Canada Eliminate Recessions?
-   Achieving the ideal outcome (shifting AD perfectly to potential GDP) is difficult in practice.
-   The Bank's best realistic outcome is to make recessions shorter and milder.
-   **Lags**: There is a delay between a policy change and its full effect on real GDP, employment, and inflation (can be months or years).
    -   If the Bank is late in recognizing a recession or inflation, its policy may not be implemented quickly enough.
    -   A poorly timed policy can be **procyclical** (increasing the severity of the business cycle) rather than **countercyclical** (reducing severity).
    -   Ex: If an expansionary policy is implemented after a recession has already ended, it can push the economy beyond potential GDP and cause inflation.

![[A0FC6178-5D70-4007-AD24-F2F6117CF382.png]]

## A Summary of How Monetary Policy Works
-   The summary isolates the effect of monetary policy, *holding constant all other factors* (ceteris paribus).
-   An expansionary policy causes real GDP and the price level to be *higher than they would otherwise have been*.
-   A contractionary policy causes real GDP and the price level to be *lower than they would otherwise have been*.
-   **Terminology**:
    -   Expansionary monetary policy: *loose* or *easy* policy.
    -   Contractionary monetary policy: *tight* policy.

![[6AC09948-C185-4760-A67C-3D88348754AE.png]]

## Don’t LetThis Happen to You
> [!ERROR] REMEMBER THAT WITH MONETARY POLICY, IT’S THE INTEREST RATES—NOT THE MONEY—THAT COUNT
> It is tempting to think that increasing the money supply directly leads to more spending because people have more money. However, when the Bank of Canada increases the money supply (e.g., by buying government securities), sellers exchange one asset (securities) for another (a cheque); their income has not directly increased.
>
> Spending is affected *only when monetary policy lowers interest rates*. Lower interest rates encourage households to buy homes and cars, and businesses to invest. They also lead to a lower value of the dollar, increasing net exports. It is the *lower interest rates* that drive this additional spending, not the mere increase in the money supply.

# Monetary Policy in the Dynamic Aggregate Demand and Aggregate2 Supply Model **not on the exam**
The previous discussion simplified monetary policy by ignoring continuous inflation and long-run economic growth. The **dynamic aggregate demand and aggregate supply model** accounts for these.

-   Over time, the Canadian labour force, capital stock, and technological change increase, causing potential GDP to rise (LRAS shifts right).
-   Firms supply more goods/services at any given price (SRAS shifts right).
-   Aggregate demand also shifts right due to:
    1.  Population growth and rising incomes (increased consumption).
    2.  Firms expanding capacity and new firms being established (increased investment).
    3.  Increased government services (increased government purchases).

## The Effects of Monetary Policy on Real GDP and the Price Level: A More Complete Account
-   Sometimes, AD does not increase enough to keep real GDP at potential GDP (e.g., due to pessimism, government spending cuts, or foreign recessions).
-   If the Bank of Canada anticipates a gap between equilibrium real GDP and potential GDP, it may implement an **expansionary monetary policy** to lower interest rates and stimulate aggregate demand.
-   A successful expansionary policy shifts AD to the right, bringing equilibrium to potential GDP. This prevents falling incomes and rising unemployment.
-   This policy also causes the inflation rate to be *higher than it would have been* without the intervention.

![[E16A96A1-5469-43C7-B24F-8FACB0A9DFAC.jpg]]

## Using Monetary Policy to Fight Inflation
-   The Bank of Canada can use a **contractionary monetary policy** to prevent aggregate demand from expanding too rapidly, which would cause the inflation rate to accelerate.
-   By raising interest rates, the Bank can limit the increase in aggregate demand, keeping real GDP at its potential level and preventing excessive price level increases.

# A Closer Look at the Bank of Canada’s Setting of Monetary Policy Targets
## Should the Central Bank Target the Money Supply?
-   Some economists (monetarists, led by Milton Friedman) argue for targeting the money supply rather than interest rates.
-   They propose a **monetary growth rule**: increasing the money supply at a constant rate (e.g., equal to the long-run annual growth rate of real GDP, about 3%) regardless of economic conditions.
-   Friedman believed active monetary policy destabilizes the economy.
-   Support for a monetary growth rule has declined since 1980 because the relationship between money supply movements and real GDP/price level has weakened.

## Why Doesn’t the Bank of Canada Target Both the Money Supply and the Interest Rate?
-   The Bank of Canada controls the money *supply*, but not money *demand*.
-   It can achieve an interest rate target OR a money supply target, but not both simultaneously, because only combinations representing equilibrium in the money market are attainable.
-   The Bank must choose between targeting an interest rate and targeting the money supply. It has primarily chosen the overnight interest rate.

![[A9935A7A-9E43-411C-AAAB-2C6BF203F51D.png]]

## The Taylor Rule
-   Developed by John Taylor, it explains how central banks set the overnight interest rate target.
-   It starts with an estimate of the **equilibrium real overnight interest rate** (the real overnight interest rate consistent with real GDP at potential GDP in the long run, typically 2%).
-   **Taylor rule**:
    $$\text{Overnight interest target rate} = \text{Inflation rate} + \text{Equilibrium real overnight interest rate} + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Inflation gap}) + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Output gap})$$
    -   **Inflation gap**: Current inflation - target inflation rate.
    -   **Output gap**: Percentage difference between real GDP and potential GDP.
-   The Taylor rule incorporates both inflation and real GDP fluctuations.
-   **Taylor principle**: When the inflation rate increases by one percentage point, the central bank increases the overnight interest rate by *more than one percentage point* (e.g., by 1.5 percentage points in the formula above) so that the real interest rate also rises, ensuring monetary policy is stabilizing.

## Solved Problem 11.2: APPLYING THE TAYLOR RULE
John Williams argued that following the Taylor rule during the 2007–2009 recession would have required pushing short-term rates deeply into negative territory. During Q2 2020, the Taylor rule indicated a federal funds rate of -3.1% when it was 0.1%.

**a. What does it mean for an interest rate to be negative?**
-   A negative interest rate means the borrower pays the lender back *less* than the amount borrowed. This is very rare for nominal interest rates. A negative *real* interest rate (inflation rate higher than nominal interest rate) occurs more frequently.

**b. Use the equation for the Taylor rule to show how the overnight interest target rate might be negative. Assume that the equilibrium real overnight interest rate and the target rate of inflation are both 2 percent and the current inflation rate is 0 percent.**
-   Taylor rule: $\text{Overnight interest target rate} = \text{Inflation rate} + \text{Equilibrium real overnight interest rate} + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Inflation gap}) + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Output gap})$
-   Given:
    -   Inflation rate = 0%
    -   Equilibrium real overnight interest rate = 2%
    -   Target inflation rate = 2%
    -   Inflation gap = 0% - 2% = -2%
-   Substitute into the rule:
    $\text{Overnight interest target rate} = 0\% + 2\% + \frac{1}{2} \times (-2\%) + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Output gap})$
    $\text{Overnight interest target rate} = 2\% - 1\% + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Output gap})$
    $\text{Overnight interest target rate} = 1\% + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Output gap})$
-   For the target rate to be negative, the output gap must be sufficiently negative. For example, if the output gap is -4%:
    $\text{Overnight interest target rate} = 1\% + \frac{1}{2} \times (-4\%) = 1\% - 2\% = -1\%$
-   This shows that a large negative output gap (economy significantly below potential) can lead the Taylor rule to suggest a negative nominal interest rate. This is known as the **zero lower bound** problem.

## Inflation Targeting and the Federal Reserve’s New Monetary Policy Strategy
-   **Inflation targeting**: A central bank publicly sets an explicit target for the inflation rate over time, and its performance is judged on hitting this target.
    -   Provides an anchor for inflationary expectations.
    -   Promotes central bank accountability.
    -   Critics argue it reduces flexibility for other policy goals and relies on potentially inaccurate inflation forecasts.
-   In 2020, the Fed adopted **average-inflation targeting**: aiming for inflation that averages 2% over time, meaning it will seek inflation moderately above 2% after periods of below-target inflation.
    -   Automatically makes monetary policy expansionary during recessions (when inflation falls) and contractionary during high inflation.
-   Some economists advocate **nominal GDP targeting**: setting a target for nominal GDP growth (e.g., 5% if real GDP growth is 3% and inflation target is 2%).
    -   Automatically expansionary during recessions and contractionary during high inflation.
    -   Critics note GDP data revisions and unfamiliarity to the public.

# Central Bank Policies during the 2007–2009 and 2020 Recessions
Traditional response to recession: lower the target for the overnight interest rate. The severity of the 2007–2009 and 2020 recessions led to additional measures due to the **zero lower bound problem** (interest rates effectively at zero, but not enough to stimulate demand).

## The Inflation and Deflation of the Housing Market Bubble in the United States
-   The Fed lowered the federal funds rate during the 2001 recession, stimulating housing demand.
-   By 2005, a housing "bubble" formed: housing prices nearly doubled (2000-2006) while rents rose less than 25%.
-   Bubbles occur when:
    1.  Investors overestimate true asset value.
    2.  Investors expect to profit by selling at even higher prices.
-   The bubble burst in 2006-2007, leading to declining home sales and prices.
-   **Subprime loans**: Loans to borrowers with flawed credit histories, which led to heavy losses for lenders and a **credit crunch** (tightened lending requirements).
-   The housing market decline affected other markets (e.g., consumption spending on durables).

## The Changing Mortgage Market in the United States
-   Before the 1970s, banks held mortgages until paid off.
-   Congress created a secondary market for mortgages to increase home ownership.
-   **Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac** (government-sponsored enterprises, similar to Canada's CMHC) bought mortgages from banks and sold **mortgage-backed securities** to investors. This allowed banks to grant more loans.

## The Role of Investment Banks in the United States
-   By the 2000s, investment banks (e.g., Morgan Stanley) became key players, bundling mortgages into mortgage-backed securities and reselling them.
-   Lenders loosened mortgage standards, anticipating continued housing price increases.
-   When housing prices declined, defaults rose, and the value of mortgage-backed securities plummeted, causing heavy losses for commercial and investment banks.

## The Federal Reserve and the US Treasury Department Respond
The Fed and Treasury partnered to address the severe financial crisis.

### Initial Fed and Treasury Actions
| Date | Action | Goal |
| :------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| March 2008 | Fed temporarily made discount loans to primary dealers (including investment banks). | Provide short-term funds to prevent dealers from selling securities, which would further reduce prices. |
| March 2008 | Fed loaned up to $200 billion of Treasury securities in exchange for mortgage-backed securities. | Allow primary dealers with illiquid mortgage-backed securities to access Treasury securities for short-term collateral. |
| March 2008 | Fed and Treasury helped JPMorgan Chase acquire failing investment bank Bear Stearns. | Avoid a financial panic from Bear Stearns' failure, which could have stopped lending to other investment banks. |
| September 2008 | Treasury took control of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (private businesses). | Avoid further devastation of the housing market and a collapse of confidence in mortgage-backed securities. |

### Responses to the Failure of Lehman Brothers
-   The Fed and Treasury weighed the **moral hazard problem** (firms taking riskier investments if they expect government bailouts) against wider economic repercussions.
-   They allowed Lehman Brothers to go bankrupt (September 15, 2008), which caused a stronger adverse reaction than expected.
-   Two days later, the Fed provided an $85 billion loan to AIG (largest US insurance company) in exchange for 80% government ownership.
-   Congress passed the **Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)**, where the US Treasury provided funds to commercial banks in exchange for stock, taking partial ownership.
-   These actions were controversial but aimed at traditional macroeconomic goals.

## Why Didn’t Canada Have a Housing Bubble and Banking Crisis in 2008?
-   Canada's banking regulator (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) was more conservative.
-   Canadian banks had lower leverage and more conservative lending/acquisition practices.
-   Canada's banking system was viewed as soundest globally.
-   However, low interest rates post-crisis led to distortions like high household debt and an overheated housing market in Canada.
-   The Minister of Finance tightened mortgage insurance rules to cool the housing market and address financial imbalances.

## The Bank of Canada Responds to the Covid-19 Recession
-   March 2020: Bank of Canada rapidly cut its policy rate to 0.25%, its **effective lower bound**.
-   Lowered the operating band for the overnight interest rate to 25 basis points and targeted the rate at the bottom of the band.
-   Enhanced standing liquidity facilities and term repo operations:
    -   Expanded eligible financial institutions for lending.
    -   Widened accepted collateral.
    -   Lengthened lending terms.
    -   Established a new *standing term liquidity facility* (March 31, 2020) for financial institutions to manage liquidity and provide loans.
-   Introduced various temporary lending facilities to businesses and provincial/local governments:

| Program | Date of Creation | Function |
| :----------------------------------------- | :--------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Canada Mortgage Bond Purchase Program | March 17, 2020 | Buys Canada mortgage bonds in the secondary market to provide funds for financial institutions to renew mortgages. |
| Bankers’ Acceptance Purchase Facility | March 23, 2020 | Buys bankers’ acceptances (BAs) in the secondary market to support the BAs market, a key funding source for small/medium corporations. |
| Provincial Money Market Purchase Program | March 25, 2020 | Purchases short-term provincial money market securities to support provincial borrowing. |
| Standing Term Liquidity Facility | March 31, 2020 | Lends to eligible financial institutions against broad collateral (including mortgages) to improve new lending. |
| Government of Canada Bond Purchase Program | April 1, 2020 | Purchases government of Canada securities in the secondary market to support market functioning and provide monetary stimulus. |
| Commercial Paper Purchase Program | April 2, 2020 | Purchases Commercial Paper (CP) to ensure normal functioning of the CP market, a key short-term financing source for firms/public authorities. |
| Contingent Term Repo Facility | April 6, 2020 | Offers one-month funding to eligible counterparties against government-guaranteed securities. |
| Provincial Bond Purchase Program | May 7, 2020 | Supplements the Provincial Money Market Purchase Program to maintain a well-functioning provincial funding market. |
| Corporate Bond Purchase Program | May 26, 2020 | Supports the proper functioning of the Canadian corporate debt market. |

-   These facilities ensured funds flowed to firms and governments, largely avoiding a credit crunch in Canada.

## Economics in Your Life and Career
> [!NOTE] SHOULD YOU BUY A HOUSE DURING A RECESSION?
> Buying a house is a major decision. During a recession, the Bank of Canada often lowers interest rates, making mortgage rates typically lower. This can be an advantage. However, recessions also bring rising unemployment. If your job security is uncertain, committing to a large, long-term loan is risky. Therefore, if your job seems secure, buying a house during a recession when interest rates are low may be a good idea.
