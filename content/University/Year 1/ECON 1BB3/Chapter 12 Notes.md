# What Is Fiscal Policy?
**Fiscal Policy**: Changes in federal taxes and spending that are intended to achieve macroeconomic policy goals.
- This is distinct from monetary policy, which is managed by the Bank of Canada.
- It also doesn't include policies with other goals, like environmental or national security policies.

## Automatic Stabilizers vs. Discretionary Fiscal Policy
**Automatic Stabilizers**: Government spending and taxes that automatically increase or decrease along with the business cycle without any new action by the government.
- **Example (Recession)**: More people become unemployed -> government spending on Employment Insurance automatically increases -> tax revenue automatically falls. This helps cushion the economic downturn.
- **Example (Expansion)**: Employment rises -> government spending on EI decreases -> tax revenue increases. This helps slow down an overheating economy.

**Discretionary Fiscal Policy**: When the government takes deliberate actions to change spending or taxes.
- **Example**: The government passing a new bill to cut taxes or increase infrastructure spending to fight a recession.

## Overview of Government Spending and Taxes
- Since the 1960s, the federal government's share of total government spending has generally declined, while provincial spending (especially on health care) has risen.
- **Government Purchases**: Spending where the government receives a good or service in return (e.g., buying fighter jets, hiring an RCMP officer).
- **Government Expenditures**: Includes purchases *plus* transfer payments (e.g., Canada Health Transfer, Old Age Security).

### Where Does the Money Go?
Federal government spending is dominated by transfers to people and other levels of government.
- **Typical Major Spending Areas**: Old Age Security, Canada Health Transfer, Employment Insurance.
- **2021 Exception**: COVID-19 support programs temporarily became the largest spending category.

![[08F26C53-BAE8-4CF7-A25E-5A65884F2029.png]]

### Where Does the Money Come From?
The federal government's revenue comes primarily from taxes.
1.  **Personal Income Taxes**: The largest source (around 50-55%).
2.  **Corporate Income Taxes**: The second-largest source (around 15-17%).
3.  **GST (Goods and Services Tax)**: The third main source (around 11%).

# The Effects of Fiscal Policy on Real GDP and the Price Level
Fiscal policy is used to influence aggregate demand (AD) to stabilize the economy.

**Expansionary Fiscal Policy**: Involves *increasing government purchases* or *decreasing taxes*.
- **Goal**: To increase aggregate demand and combat a recession.
- **How it works**:
    - Increasing government purchases directly increases AD.
    - Decreasing taxes increases households' disposable income, which indirectly increases consumption spending (C) and thus AD.
- **Result**: Real GDP and the price level rise.

**Contractionary Fiscal Policy**: Involves *decreasing government purchases* or *increasing taxes*.
- **Goal**: To decrease aggregate demand and combat rising inflation.
- **How it works**:
    - Decreasing government purchases directly decreases AD.
    - Increasing taxes reduces disposable income, which decreases consumption spending (C) and thus AD.
- **Result**: Real GDP and the price level fall (or rise more slowly than they would have otherwise).

![[A84C3FE5-9EC3-4335-90E2-879A97655572.png]]

| Problem | Type of Policy | Action by Government | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recession | Expansionary | Increase government spending or cut taxes | Real GDP and the price level rise. |
| Rising inflation | Contractionary | Decrease government spending or raise taxes | Real GDP and the price level fall. |

## Fiscal Policy in the Dynamic AD-AS Model
In a realistic, growing economy, the goal of fiscal policy is to manage the shifts in the AD curve to keep the economy at potential GDP.
- **Expansionary Policy**: Used when AD is not increasing enough to keep up with the growth in LRAS. The policy shifts AD further to the right to reach the new long-run equilibrium.
- **Contractionary Policy**: Used when AD is increasing too much, pushing the economy beyond potential GDP and causing high inflation. The policy reduces the shift in AD to bring the economy back to the long-run equilibrium with a lower price level.

# The Government Purchases and Tax Multipliers
**Multiplier Effect**: The series of induced increases in consumption spending that results from an initial increase in autonomous expenditures.
- An initial increase in government spending of $10 billion leads to a total increase in equilibrium real GDP of *more* than $10 billion.
- **Autonomous spending**: The initial government spending, which doesn't depend on the level of real GDP.
- **Induced spending**: The subsequent increases in consumption spending that result from the increased income generated by the autonomous spending.

### Multiplier Formulas
**Government Purchases Multiplier**: The ratio of the change in equilibrium real GDP to the initial change in government purchases.
$$ \text{Government purchases multiplier} = \frac{\text{Change in equilibrium real GDP}}{\text{Change in government purchases}} $$

**Tax Multiplier**: The ratio of the change in equilibrium real GDP to the change in taxes.
$$ \text{Tax multiplier} = \frac{\text{Change in equilibrium real GDP}}{\text{Change in taxes}} $$

> [!NOTE] Title
> The tax multiplier is always a negative number because a tax *cut* increases GDP, and a tax *hike* decreases GDP. Its absolute value is smaller than the government purchases multiplier because households will save a portion of a tax cut, so not all of it gets spent in the first round.

### The Effect of Aggregate Supply
The simple multiplier effect assumes a constant price level. However, because the SRAS curve is upward-sloping, an increase in AD also leads to a higher price level. This increase in the price level reduces the total quantity of goods demanded, making the *actual* increase in real GDP smaller than what the simple multiplier would predict.

# The Limits of Fiscal Policy
## Timing Lags
Getting the timing right for discretionary fiscal policy is difficult.
1.  **Legislative Lag**: It takes a long time for the government (Prime Minister and Parliament) to agree on and pass new fiscal policy legislation. This is much slower than monetary policy decisions made by the Bank of Canada.
2.  **Implementation Lag**: After a policy is approved, it takes time to implement. For example, a new highway project requires planning, bidding, and hiring before any money is actually spent.

Poorly timed policy can make the economy worse—for example, an expansionary policy might take effect just as the economy is already recovering, leading to excessive inflation.

## Crowding Out
**Crowding Out**: A decline in private expenditures (consumption, investment, net exports) as a result of an increase in government purchases.

- **Short-Run Crowding Out**:
    1.  Government increases spending to stimulate the economy.
    2.  This increases real GDP and income, which increases the demand for money.
    3.  With a fixed money supply, the interest rate rises.
    4.  The higher interest rate "crowds out" private spending:
        - Firms reduce investment (I).
        - Households reduce spending on durable goods (C).
        - The Canadian dollar appreciates, reducing net exports (NX).
    - The result is that the expansionary fiscal policy is less effective than it would be without crowding out.

- **Long-Run Crowding Out**: In the long run, an increase in government's share of GDP must come at the expense of the private sector's share. Most economists believe that a permanent increase in government spending results in **complete crowding out**, meaning private spending falls by the same amount that government spending increased.

# Deficits, Surpluses, and Federal Government Debt
**Budget Deficit**: When the federal government’s expenditures are greater than its tax revenue.
**Budget Surplus**: When the federal government’s expenditures are less than its tax revenue.
**Federal Government Debt**: The total value of government bonds outstanding, which is equal to the sum of all past budget deficits minus any budget surpluses.

![[3984E11A-002A-4667-8D53-72966F742D86.png]]

## The Budget as an Automatic Stabilizer
During a recession, the budget automatically moves toward a deficit:
1.  Wages and profits fall, so tax revenues decrease.
2.  Government spending on transfer payments (like EI) increases.
This automatic "tax cut" and spending increase helps to cushion the recession without any discretionary action.

**Cyclically Adjusted Budget Deficit or Surplus**: The deficit or surplus in the federal government’s budget if the economy were at potential GDP. This measure helps to separate the effects of automatic stabilizers from the effects of discretionary fiscal policy.

> [!IMPORTANT] Title
> Few economists believe the budget should be balanced every single year. Trying to balance the budget during a recession would require raising taxes or cutting spending, which would make the recession worse.

## Is Government Debt a Problem?
- A large debt can be a problem if interest payments become a significant portion of government spending, forcing tax hikes or cuts to other programs.
- A rising debt-to-GDP ratio can also lead to crowding out of private investment, which can slow long-term economic growth.
- However, if the debt was used to finance long-term investments in infrastructure or education, it can enhance the economy's productive capacity, offsetting some of the negative effects.

# The Effects of Fiscal Policy in the Long Run
Some fiscal policies, known as **supply-side economics**, are designed to affect aggregate supply rather than aggregate demand, with the goal of promoting long-run economic growth.

**Tax Wedge**: The difference between the pre-tax and post-tax return to an economic activity.
- A smaller tax wedge (i.e., lower marginal tax rates) increases the incentive to work, save, invest, and start businesses.

### Long-Run Effects of Tax Policy
- **Individual Income Tax Cuts**: Reducing marginal tax rates increases the return to working and saving, which can increase the supply of labour and loanable funds.
- **Corporate Income Tax Cuts**: Increases the after-tax return on investment, encouraging firms to invest more in capital, which can increase the pace of technological change.
- **Tax Simplification**: A simpler tax code would reduce compliance costs and distortions, freeing up economic resources for more productive uses.

![[2AC8682D-C0D0-4019-B641-0B37011B0987.png]]

> [!NOTE] Title
> The main debate among economists is not *if* supply-side effects exist, but *how large* they are. Skeptics argue that the effects on aggregate supply are small and that tax cuts primarily affect aggregate demand.
