## Introduction to Key Economic Concepts

*   **Business Cycle**: Alternating periods of economic expansion and recession.
    *   **Expansion**: A period during which total production and total employment are increasing.
    *   **Recession**: A period during which total production and total employment are decreasing.
*   **Economic Growth**: The ability for an economy to produce increasing quantities of goods and services.
*   **Inflation Rate**: The percentage increase in the average level of prices from one year to the next.

---

## Understanding Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

### Definition of GDP
**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**: The *market value of all final goods & services produced* in a region (country) in a given period of time (year).
*   Measures production with market values, not quantities. **Don't compare apples to oranges, compare dollars to dollars.**
*   Only includes current production **(produced in the same year).**
*   Does not include used goods (e.g., a new textbook ✅, but selling it to a friend 🚫).
- Produced in one country
### Key Concepts for GDP Calculation
*   **Final good or service**: One that is purchased by its final user and is not included in the production of any other good or service (e.g., hamburgers).
*   **Intermediate goods**: A good or service that is an input into another good or service (e.g., car seats).

> [!NOTE]
> In calculating GDP, we include the value of the car but not the separate value of the car seats. If we included the value of the car seats, we would be _double counting_. As a result, we **must use the value of the final sale**

## The Circular Flow of Income and Expenditure

This model illustrates the equality between total production, total income, and total expenditure in an economy.
$$\text{value of total production} =\text{value of total income} = \text{total expenditure}$$

When you buy something, the money you spend doesn’t disappear — it becomes someone else’s income.

Example:
- You pay **$450** for an iPad.
- Some of that goes to the store (profit).
- Some goes to the salesperson (wages).
- Some goes to Apple (profit).
- Apple pays its workers and suppliers (wages + profits).
    
In the end, **all the money spent on goods and services becomes income for someone** (workers, businesses, or government through taxes).

### Factors of Production and Income Distribution
*   Firms use the *factors of production* (labour, capital, natural resources, entrepreneurship) to produce goods and services.
*   Households supply the factors of production to firms in exchange for income.
*   Income is divided into: Wages (for labour), interest (for capital), rent (for natural resources), and profit (for entrepreneurship).
*   **Profit** is the income that remains after a firm has paid wages, interest, and rent.

### Transfer Payments
**Transfer payments**: Payments by the government to households for which the government *does not receive a new good or service in return*.
*   Include social program payments (like Old Age Security and Guaranteed Income Supplement).
*   Includes employment insurance payments.
*   **NOT INCLUDED IN GDP** because there is no exchange of a new good or service.

## Methods of Calculating GDP

There are three main ways to calculate GDP: the expenditure approach, the income approach, and the value-added method.

![[Pasted image 20250921141155.png]]

### The Expenditure Approach
This approach sums up all the spending on domestically produced goods and services.

$$Y = C+I+G+Nx$$

Where:
*   **Y** - GDP (or total output/income)
*   **C** - Consumption (the final consumption expenditure done by households). This is the *largest component* of GDP.
    *   **Household consumption**: spending on food, clothing, etc. (*over half of GDP is household consumption alone*).
    *   **Government consumption**: spending on services like health care, education, defense.
    *   **Not-for-profit organizations**: small portion, spending to serve households.
*   **I** - Investment (Gross Fixed Capital Formation + Investment in Inventories). The money spent by firms on:
    *   **Gross Fixed Capital Formation**: Purchases of *fixed assets (capital)* by firms, governments, and households (e.g., buildings, houses, machinery). Basically: long-term investment in buildings, equipment, and infrastructure.
    *   **Investment in Inventories**: Changes in finished products kept on hand to sell or inputs to turn into finished products. If inventories rise → counted as investment. If inventories fall → reduces GDP contribution.
*   **G** - Government Spending (The final consumption spending and fixed capital formation done by government).
    *   **Not transfer payments** (such as welfare, Canada Pension Plan).
    *   Proper examples include RCAF, teachers' salaries, and highway repairs.
*   **Nx** - Net Exports (Exports – Imports).
    *   **Exports**: Goods and services produced in Canada and sold abroad (added to GDP).
    *   **Imports**: Goods and services produced abroad but bought in Canada (subtracted from GDP).
    *   Example (2021): Exports = 30.8% of GDP, Imports = 30.6% of GDP. **Net exports = 2.8% of GDP (positive).**

**Statistical Discrepancy**: One-half of the difference between the estimates of GDP generated by the expenditure approach and the income approach. *Differences arise due to small errors in the original data and different estimation techniques.*

### The Income Approach
This approach sums up all the income received by Canadians as the result of production that takes place in Canada. This is also referred to as **Gross Domestic Income**.
Income is divided into:
1.  **Compensation of Employees**: All the money workers get for their labour.
    *   **Wages & salaries** → regular pay for work (86.3% of employee compensation = 44% of GDP).
    *   **Employers’ social contributions** → money employers pay into programs for workers, like CPP, EI, or health plans (13.7% of employee compensation = 7% of GDP).
    *   **Half of GDP comes from paying workers — mostly wages, plus some benefits.**
2.  **Gross Operating Surplus**: Payments made to the owners of capital by firms and government for the use of their capital.
    *   Broken down into: Operating surplus of corporations, consumption of fixed capital by corporations, and consumption of fixed capital by governments or non-profit organizations.
    *   **Net operating surplus**: Payments to capital owners over and above compensation for the *consumption of their capital* (depreciation).
    *   **Consumption of Capital**: Refers to the depreciation of capital (wear and tear).
3.  **Gross Mixed Income**: Income generated by small businesses where owners often work, making it hard to separate employee payments from capital ownership payments.
4.  **Taxes less Subsidies**: Payments to government that mimic income received by the owners of other inputs.

### The Value-Added Method
This method sums the "value added" at each stage of production.
![[Pasted image 20250921145248.png]]
**Value Added**: The additional market value a firm gives a product, equal to the difference between the price the firm paid for intermediate goods and the price for which it sells the finished product.
*   Ex: Get iPhone parts for $200. Assemble it, sell for $2000. Value added = $1800.

## Does GDP Measure What We Want It to Measure?

GDP is good at measuring **production**, but it’s not perfect. Economists also sometimes use GDP as a measure of **well-being**, but it only gives a **rough picture**.

### Shortcomings of GDP as a Measure of Total Production
*   **Household Production**: Goods/services people make for themselves (like childcare, cleaning, cooking, DIY projects) are not counted. If these tasks are outsourced, they _are_ counted, even if total production hasn't truly changed.
*   **Informal Economy**: Transactions hidden from the government (illegal goods/services, tax evasion, avoiding regulation) are excluded.
    *   Canada’s informal economy is estimated at 13% of official GDP ($295B in 2018).

### Shortcomings of GDP as a Measure of Well-being
GDP doesn't fully capture the overall well-being or quality of life in a country because it doesn't account for:
*   **Leisure Time**: An increase in working hours might boost GDP but reduce quality of life.
*   **Environmental Quality**: Economic growth can come at the cost of environmental degradation, which GDP doesn't subtract.
*   **Income Distribution**: A high GDP doesn't mean everyone is better off; it doesn't show how income is distributed.
*   **Non-Market Activities**: Volunteer work, community service, and other unpaid contributions to society are not included.
*   **Product Quality Improvements**: It's hard to fully capture improvements in the quality of goods and services over time.

## Real vs Nominal GDP

We need a way of separating price changes from quantity changes to get a true picture of production growth.

### Real vs Nominal GDP
*   **Nominal GDP**: Calculated by summing the *current* values of final goods and services. It reflects both price and quantity changes.
*   **Real GDP**: Calculated by designating a particular year as the *base year* and then using the prices of goods and services in the base year to calculate the value of goods and services in all other years.
    *   By holding prices constant, changes in real GDP represent changes in the quantity of goods and services produced, not changes in prices.
    *   **Drawback**: Prices may change relative to each other over long periods, distorting comparisons.
    *   **Solution: Chain-weighted prices**: Uses *average prices* from two years (current year + previous year) instead of only base-year prices, reducing distortion.

> [!NOTE]
> Real GDP holds prices constant, which makes it a better measure than nominal GDP of changes in the production of goods and services from one year to the next.

### The GDP Deflator
*   **Price Level**: Measures the average prices of goods and services in the economy.
*   **GDP Deflator**: Tracks the overall price level (inflation/deflation) over time.
    $$\text{GDP Deflator} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} * 100 $$
*   If prices rise faster than production → nominal GDP grows faster than real GDP → GDP deflator increases (indicating inflation).

## Other Measures of Total Production and Income

**National income accounting**: Methods government agencies use to track total production and income in the economy. In Canada, these are called the National Economic Accounts.

In addition to GDP, Statistics Canada also calculates the following measures of production and income: 

### Household Income
Income received by households.
*   Calculated by starting with **Net National Income (NNI)**, then:
    *   **Subtracting**: Corporate earnings kept by firms (not paid out as dividends).
    *   **Adding**: Government transfer payments (e.g., CERB, EI) + interest households earn on government bonds.
*   Typically, Household income < NNI, but exceptions occur (e.g., 2020 COVID-19 transfers).

### Household Disposable Income
$$\text{Household Disposable Income} = \text{Household Income}- \text{Personal tax payments}$$

**Gross Domestic Income**: This is essentially another name for the income approach to calculating GDP, representing the sum of all income payments to households (wages, interest, rent, and profits) generated from production within the country.

These various measures (Household Income, Household Disposable Income) provide a more nuanced understanding of economic activity and how income flows through different sectors of the economy. While GDP focuses on total production within a country's borders, these household income measures focus on the actual funds available to individuals and families, offering different perspectives on economic health and living standards.
