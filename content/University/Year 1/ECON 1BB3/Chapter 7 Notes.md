# Economic Growth over Time and around the World
**Economic growth**: A growing economy produces both increasing quantities of goods and services and better goods and services, leading to an increase in living standards.

## **Economic Growth from 1 000 000 BCE to the Present**
*   **1 000 000 BCE - 1300 CE**: No sustained economic growth. Real GDP per capita remained around \$150 per year (2021 dollars), the minimum for survival.
*   **Industrial Revolution**: Started in England around 1750, marked by the production of cotton cloth in factories using machinery powered by steam engines, leading to sustained economic growth.
*   **Long-run economic growth**: The process by which rising productivity increases the average standard of living.
*   **Global Growth Rates**:
    *   Prior to 1300: Essentially zero.
    *   1300-1800: Very slow (0.2% per year).
    *   19th Century: Significant growth due to Industrial Revolution.
    *   20th Century: Accelerated (1.3% to 2.3% per year).
    *   Early 21st Century (2000-2019): Deceleration (1.7% per year) due to 2007-2009 recession and weak recovery.
    *   2020: Negative growth due to COVID-19.

## **Small Differences in Growth Rates Are Important**
*   **Compounding**: A process where small differences in interest rates (or growth rates) magnify over long periods of time.
*   Small differences in growth rates, when compounded over long periods, lead to large differences in living standards.
    *   Example: \$100 at 2.3% interest grows to \$312 in 50 years, while at 1.7% it grows to \$232.
*   **Real-world impact**: Countries like Türkiye (2.9% growth) saw significantly higher real GDP per capita by 2019 compared to Ghana (0.4% growth) and Mexico (1.9% growth), despite similar starting points in 1960.

## **The Problem with Slow Economic Growth**
Slow growth means failure to raise living standards, leading to persistent poverty, high infant mortality, and lower life expectancy compared to high-income countries.

## **Don’t Confuse the Average Annual Percentage Change with the Total Percentage Change**
*   **Average annual percentage changes**: The average rate of change over a period of more than one year, used by economists when discussing growth rates.
*   These are *not* total percentage changes.

## **The Variation in per Capita Income around the World**
Economies are categorized into:
1.  **High-income countries**: Also called industrial countries or developed countries, including Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand, the United States, and the countries of Western Europe.
2.  **Lower-income countries**: Also called developing countries, which include most of the countries of Africa, Asia, and Latin America.
3.  **Newly industrializing countries**: A small group of countries, mostly East Asian (e.g., Singapore and South Korea), that began to experience high rates of growth in the 1980s and 1990s.

Global GDP per capita in 2020 ranged from \$173,688 (Monaco) to \$238 (Burundi).

## **Is Income All That Matters?**
While income is crucial for improving living standards, other measures like health, education, and civil liberties have shown significant progress even in poorer countries, often due to inexpensive technological advancements (e.g., vaccines, mosquito nets) and changes in attitudes (e.g., valuing girls' education). However, sustained income growth is ultimately necessary to close the gap with high-income countries.

# **What Determines How Fast Economies Grow?**
**LO 7.2** Use the economic growth model to explain why growth rates differ across countries.

*   **Economic growth model**: Explains growth rates in real GDP per capita over the long run.
*   **Labour productivity**: The quantity of goods and services that can be produced by one worker or by one hour of work.
*   The economic growth model focuses on the causes of long-run increases in labour productivity.

## **Key Factors Determining Labour Productivity**
1.  **Quantity of capital available to workers**:
    *   **Physical capital**: Manufactured goods that are used to produce other goods and services (e.g., computers, factory buildings, machine tools, warehouses, and trucks).
    *   **Human capital**: The accumulated knowledge and skills that workers acquire from education and training or from their life experiences.
2.  **Level of technology**:
    *   **Technological change**: A change in the quantity of output firms can produce using a given quantity of inputs.
    *   **Sources of technological change**:
        *   **Better machinery and equipment**: New inventions (e.g., steam engine, computers).
        *   **Increases in human capital**: Education and training.
        *   **Better means of organizing and managing production**: (e.g., **just-in-time system**).
            *   **Just-in-time system**: A production system, first developed by Toyota Motor Corporation, that involves assembling goods from parts that arrive at the factory at exactly the time workers need them.

> [!NOTE]
> Technological change is *not* simply adding more of the same physical capital; it involves new or more productive capital, or better organization.

## **The Per-Worker Production Function**
*   **Per-worker production function**: The relationship between real GDP per hour worked and capital per hour worked, *holding the level of technology constant*.
*   **Equation**:
    *   Real GDP per hour worked: $Y/L$
    *   Capital per hour worked: $K/L$
*   **Diminishing Returns to Capital**: When technology is held constant, equal increases in capital per hour worked lead to *diminishing* increases in output per hour worked. This is due to the **law of diminishing returns**.
    *   **Law of diminishing returns**: States that as we add more of one input (e.g., capital) to a fixed quantity of another input (e.g., labour), output increases by smaller additional amounts.

![[Attachment.png]]
*Figure 7.3: The Per-Worker Production Function*

## **Technological Change: The Key to Sustaining Economic Growth**
*   Technological change shifts the production function *up*, allowing an economy to produce more real GDP per hour worked with the same quantity of capital per hour worked.
*   This helps economies avoid diminishing returns to capital.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In the long run, a country will experience an increasing standard of living only if it experiences continuing technological change.

## **New Growth Theory**
*   **New growth theory**: A model developed by Paul Romer that provides a more complete explanation of the sources of technological change that underlie productivity growth, arguing that individuals and firms respond to economic incentives affecting technological change.
*   **Knowledge capital**: The accumulated knowledge, or stock of knowledge, which is a key determinant of economic growth.
*   **Increasing returns**: A situation where, at the level of the entire economy, knowledge capital leads to proportionally larger increases in output because knowledge, once discovered, becomes available to everyone.
*   **Rival**: A good is rival if one firm's use of it prevents other firms from using it (e.g., physical capital).
*   **Excludable**: A good is excludable if the firm that owns the capital can keep other firms from using it (e.g., physical capital).
*   **Non-rival**: The use of knowledge capital is non-rival because one firm’s using that knowledge does not prevent another firm from using it.
*   **Non-excludable**: Knowledge capital is non-excludable because once known, it becomes widely available for everyone to use.
*   **Free ride**: Firms free ride when they benefit from the results of research and development they did not pay for. This leads to an inefficiently small amount of R&D.
*   **Government Policies to Increase Knowledge Capital**:
    1.  **Protecting intellectual property**: Governments grant **patents** and copyrights.
        *   **Patent**: Gives a firm the exclusive legal right to a new product for a period of 20 years from the date the patent application is filed with the government.
        *   **Trade secret**: Keeping research results confidential rather than patenting them to avoid public disclosure.
    2.  **Supporting research and development**: Government funding, grants, and tax benefits for R&D.
    3.  **Subsidizing education**: Increases the number of technically trained workers.

## **Joseph Schumpeter and Creative Destruction**
*   **Creative destruction**: Joseph Schumpeter's view that new products unleash a “gale of creative destruction” that drives older products—and, often, the firms that produced them—out of the market.
*   Entrepreneurs are central to economic growth by exploiting new technological possibilities.

# **Economic Growth in Canada**
**LO 7.3** Discuss fluctuations in productivity growth in Canada.

## **Average Annual Growth Rates in Real GDP per Person in Canada**
*   **1800-1900**: 1.8% per year (Industrial Revolution, increased capital, new technologies).
*   **Early 20th Century**: Faster growth (institutionalized technological change, R&D facilities, university research).
*   **1950-1973**: Even faster growth (post-WWII acceleration).
*   **1974-1995**: Slowdown (growth rate more than 1 percentage point lower).
*   **Mid-1990s**: Pickup (**information technology** revolution).
    *   **Information technology (IT)**: Includes increased use of computers, software, cellphones, and related innovations.
*   **Since 2006**: Slowed again.

## **Is Canada Headed for a Long Period of Slow Growth?**
Economists have two main views:
1.  **Optimistic View**: Productivity growth is difficult to measure, and Canada will likely experience high rates in the long run, driven by continuing IT advances and AI as a **general-purpose technology**.
    *   **General-purpose technology**: A technology that has the potential to affect many sectors of the economy (e.g., artificial intelligence).
    *   **Productivity J-curve**: A phenomenon where productivity initially declines when a general-purpose technology is introduced (downward part of the J) before rising significantly (upward part of the J) as businesses develop new processes to integrate it.
2.  **Pessimistic View**: Productivity growth entered a long-run decline in the mid-1970s, with IT gains being brief. Future growth rates are likely to remain low due to factors like an aging population, declining educational achievement, and increased regulations/taxes.

### **Measurement Issues**
*   Growth in services (e.g., ATMs, social media, search engines) is harder to measure than goods, potentially understating true GDP and productivity growth.

### **"Secular Stagnation"**
*   **Secular stagnation**: An extended period of slow growth, predicted by Alvin Hansen in the 1930s and revived by Lawrence Summers.
*   Economists forecasting secular stagnation believe real GDP may grow slowly due to insufficient demand for investment spending.
*   **Reasons for low investment demand**:
    1.  Slowing population growth reduces housing demand.
    2.  Modern IT firms require less capital than older industrial firms.
    3.  Falling price of capital (especially IT goods).
*   This could lead to very low (possibly negative) real interest rates and slow GDP growth.

### **. . . Or a Return to Faster Growth?**
Critics argue that low investment demand was temporary due to the 2007-2009 recession. They point to increased investment post-2018 and potential increases in demand for Canadian goods from other growing economies.

# **Why Isn’t the Whole World Rich?**
**LO 7.4** Explain economic catch-up and discuss why many poor countries have not experienced rapid economic growth.

*   The economic growth model predicts that poor countries should grow faster than rich countries (known as **catch-up** or **convergence**) because the profitability of using additional capital or better technology is greater in developing countries.
    *   **Catch-up (or convergence)**: The prediction that poor countries will grow faster than rich countries, leading to convergence in levels of GDP per capita.

## **Catch-up: Sometimes But Not Always**
*   **Among High-Income Countries**: Catch-up has occurred. Countries with lower incomes in 1960 (e.g., Korea, Singapore) grew fastest, while those with highest incomes (e.g., Switzerland, US) grew slowest.

    *   **Negative economic growth**: A situation where real GDP per capita decreases over time.

## **Why Haven’t Most Western European Countries, Canada, and Japan Caught Up to the United States?**
Since 1990, many high-income countries have fallen further behind the US in real GDP per capita.
*   **Reasons**:
    1.  **Greater flexibility of US labour markets**: Less restrictive regulations, higher job mobility, and lower unemployment benefits encourage faster job matching and higher productivity.
    2.  **Greater efficiency of the US financial system**: High legal protection for investors, liquid financial markets (**liquidity**), and the role of **venture capital firms** in funding technology startups give the US an advantage in implementing new technologies.
        *   **Liquidity**: The ease with which one asset can be converted into a different asset, usually for exchanging a financial asset for money.
        *   **Venture capital firms**: Firms that raise funds from institutional investors and wealthy individuals to invest in new firms ("startups"), particularly those based on new technologies.

## **Why Don’t More Low-Income Countries Experience Rapid Growth?**
Four key factors hinder growth in many low-income countries:
1.  **Failure to enforce the rule of law**: Lack of secure **property rights** and inability to enforce contracts due to **corruption** or weak legal systems discourages business and investment.
    *   **Property rights**: The rights of individuals or firms to own and control their property.
    *   **Rule of law**: The ability of a government to enforce the laws of the country, particularly with respect to protecting private property and enforcing contracts.
    *   **Corruption**: Government officials using their positions for personal gain, often undermining the rule of law and property rights.
2.  **Wars and revolutions**: Political instability makes it difficult to accumulate capital or adopt new technologies.
3.  **Poor public education and health**: Low **human capital** due to weak school systems, illiteracy, and prevalent diseases (e.g., malaria, AIDS) reduces worker productivity.
4.  **Low rates of saving and investment**: A **vicious cycle of poverty** where low incomes lead to low savings, which means few funds are available for firms to invest, leading to slow economic growth and thus continued low incomes and savings.
    *   **Vicious cycle of poverty**: A situation where low incomes lead to low savings, which leads to low investment, slow economic growth, and thus continued low incomes and savings.

## **The Benefits of Globalization**
*   **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**: Occurs when a firm builds or purchases a facility in a foreign country.
*   **Foreign portfolio investment**: Occurs when an individual or a firm buys stocks or bonds issued in another country.
*   **Globalization**: The process of countries becoming more open to foreign trade and investment.
*   FDI and foreign portfolio investment can give a low-income country access to technology and funds otherwise unavailable. Countries that embraced globalization have generally grown faster.

# **Growth Policies**
**LO 7.5** Discuss government policies that foster economic growth.

Governments can implement policies to promote long-run economic growth:

## **Enhancing Property Rights and the Rule of Law**
*   Enforcing **property rights** and contracts is fundamental for a market system to function.
*   Combating government **corruption** and ensuring political stability are prerequisites for economic growth.

## **Improving Health and Education**
*   **Health**: Improved health leads to more productive workers. Initiatives like vaccinations and better sanitation reduce illness and death.
*   **Education**: Subsidizing education increases **human capital**. Removing restrictions on girls' education and women's employment can also boost growth.
*   **Brain drain**: Refers to highly educated and successful individuals leaving developing countries for high-income countries. Rapid economic growth can reduce this.

## **Policies That Promote Technological Change**
*   **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: Relaxing regulations on FDI allows low-income countries to access foreign technology.
*   **Subsidizing R&D**: Governments can conduct research, provide grants to universities, and offer tax breaks to firms.
*   **Patents** and copyrights: Protect intellectual property, incentivizing innovation.

## **Policies That Promote Saving and Investment**
*   **Tax incentives for saving**: (e.g., Registered Retirement Savings Plans (RRSPs) in Canada) increase the return on savings.
*   **Investment tax credits**: Government policies that allow firms to deduct from their taxes some fraction of the funds they have spent on investment.
*   **Reduced taxes on profits**: Increase the after-tax return on investments.

## **Is Economic Growth Good or Bad?**
While economic growth is generally seen as desirable, especially for low-income countries, concerns exist:
*   **Environmental impact**: Growth may contribute to global warming, resource depletion, and pollution.
*   **Globalization effects**: Some argue it undermines distinctive cultures and raises ethical concerns about multinational firms' practices in low-income countries (e.g., low wages, lax regulations).
