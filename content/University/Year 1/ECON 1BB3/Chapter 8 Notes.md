## The Aggregate Expenditure Model

**Aggregate Expenditure (AE) Model**: A macroeconomic model that focuses on the short-run relationship between total spending and real GDP.
*   **Key Idea**: In any particular year, the level of real GDP is determined mainly by the level of aggregate expenditure.
*   **Central Assumption**: The price level is constant. This means we don't have to worry about the difference between real and nominal GDP.

### Components of Aggregate Expenditure
Planned Aggregate Expenditure ($AE$) is the total planned spending in an economy.
$$AE = C + I + G + NX$$


> [!NOTE] Title
> In a closed economy, $NX = 0$

*   **Consumption ($C$)**: Spending by households on goods and services.
*   **Planned Investment ($I$)**: Planned spending by firms on capital goods (factories, machines) and by households on new homes.
*   **Government Purchases ($G$)**: Spending by all levels of government on goods and services (e.g., infrastructure, salaries).
*   **Net Exports ($NX$)**: Spending by foreign firms and households on Canadian goods and services minus spending by Canadian firms and households on foreign goods and services.

### Planned Investment vs. Actual Investment
The difference between planned and actual investment comes from unplanned changes in inventories.
*   **Inventories**: Products that have been produced but not yet sold.
*   **Unplanned Increase in Inventories**: Occurs when firms sell less than they expected. Actual investment will be *greater* than planned investment.
*   **Unplanned Decrease in Inventories**: Occurs when firms sell more than they expected. Actual investment will be *less* than planned investment.
*   Actual investment equals planned investment only when there is no unplanned change in inventories.

### Macroeconomic Equilibrium
Macroeconomic equilibrium occurs where total spending equals total production.
$$\text{Planned Aggregate Expenditure} = \text{GDP}$$
$$AE = Y$$
The economy adjusts to equilibrium through firms' responses to unplanned inventory changes, not through price adjustments (since we assume the price level is constant).

| If... | then... | and... |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Planned AE = GDP | Inventories don’t change | The economy is in macroeconomic equilibrium. |
| Planned AE < GDP | Inventories rise | GDP and employment decrease. |
| Planned AE > GDP | Inventories fall | GDP and employment increase. |

## Determinants of Aggregate Expenditure
### Consumption ($C$)
Consumption is the largest component of aggregate expenditure and generally follows a smooth, upward trend, only declining during recessions.
![[Pasted Graphic.png]]

The five most important determinants of consumption are:
1.  **Current Disposable Income**: The most important determinant. Higher disposable income leads to more spending.
2.  **Household Wealth**: A household’s assets minus its liabilities. When wealth increases (e.g., stock prices rise), consumption tends to increase.
3.  **Expected Future Income**: People prefer stable consumption. If they expect higher future income (like students), they may spend more now.
4.  **The Price Level**: A higher price level reduces the real value of household wealth, causing consumption to fall. A lower price level has the opposite effect.
5.  **The Interest Rate**: A higher real interest rate increases the reward for saving, which tends to decrease spending, especially on durable goods financed by borrowing (e.g., cars, furniture).

### The Consumption Function
**Consumption Function**: The relationship between consumption spending and disposable income.
*   **Autonomous Consumption**: The level of consumption that occurs even when disposable income is zero. It is financed out of savings and represents the intercept of the consumption function.
*   **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)**: The slope of the consumption function; the amount by which consumption spending changes when disposable income changes.
    $$\text{MPC} = \frac{\text{Change in Consumption}}{\text{Change in Disposable Income}} = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y_D}$$
*   The change in consumption can also be calculated as:
    $$\text{Change in Consumption} = \text{Change in Disposable Income} \times \text{MPC}$$

The formula is: $C = a + MPC * (Yd)$
Where:

- **a** is autonomous consumption (spending when income is zero).
- **MPC** is the marginal propensity to consume,.
- **Yd** is disposable income (income after taxes). The formula is **Yd = Y - T**.
### Income, Consumption, and Saving
Households either spend their income, save it, or use it to pay taxes.
*   **Relationship between National Income and Disposable Income**:
    $$\text{Disposable Income} = \text{National Income} - \text{Net Taxes}$$
    $$\text{National Income} = \text{Disposable Income} + \text{Net Taxes}$$
    *(Where Net Taxes = Taxes - Government Transfer Payments)*
*   For the economy as a whole:
    $$\text{National Income} = \text{Consumption} + \text{Saving} + \text{Taxes}$$
    $$Y = C + S + T$$
*   Assuming taxes are constant, a change in national income is divided between consumption and saving:
    $$\text{Change in National Income} = \text{Change in Consumption} + \text{Change in Saving}$$
    $$\Delta Y = \Delta C + \Delta S$$
*   **Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)**: The amount by which saving changes when disposable income changes.
    $$\text{MPS} = \frac{\text{Change in Saving}}{\text{Change in Disposable Income}} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y_D}$$

> [!important]
> The MPC and MPS must sum to 1, because any additional dollar of income must be either consumed or saved (assuming taxes are constant).
> $$1 = MPC + MPS$$

### Planned Investment ($I$)
Investment is more volatile than consumption and declines significantly during recessions.
![[Pasted Graphic 13.png]]

> [!NOTE] How to find it
> Look at the difference between the consumption function $C$ and consumption plus planned investment line $(C + I)$.

The four most important determinants of investment are:
1.  **Expectations of Future Profits**: Firms invest when they are optimistic about future demand and profitability.
2.  **Interest Rate**: A higher real interest rate makes borrowing more expensive, leading to less investment spending.
3.  **Taxes**: Corporate income taxes and investment tax incentives affect the after-tax profitability of investments.
4.  **Cash Flow**: The difference between a firm's cash revenues and cash spending. Higher profits lead to greater cash flow, which can be used to finance investment.

### Government Purchases ($G$)
Includes spending by federal, provincial, and local governments on goods and services. It does not include transfer payments.
![[Pasted Graphic 14.png]]

### Net Exports ($NX$)
Net exports equal exports minus imports.
![[Pasted Graphic 15.png]]
The three most important determinants of net exports are:
1.  **The Price Level in Canada vs. Other Countries**: If Canadian inflation is lower, our goods become cheaper, increasing exports and decreasing imports (NX rises).
2.  **The Growth Rate of GDP in Canada vs. Other Countries**: If Canadian GDP grows faster, our imports rise more than our exports, causing NX to fall.
3.  **The Exchange Rate**: A stronger Canadian dollar makes our exports more expensive and imports cheaper, causing NX to fall.

## Graphing Macroeconomic Equilibrium
The slope of the aggregate expenditure function is

$$
\frac{\Delta\text{AE}}{\Delta\text{Real GDP}}
$$

### The 45°-Line Diagram
We use a 45°-line diagram (or Keynesian cross) to illustrate macroeconomic equilibrium.
*   The **45° line** shows all points where planned aggregate expenditure equals real GDP ($AE = Y$).
*   The **Aggregate Expenditure (AE) function** is an upward-sloping line showing the amount of planned AE at every level of GDP. Its slope is the MPC.
*   **Equilibrium** occurs where the AE function intersects the 45° line.

![[Pasted Graphic 19.png]]

*   **If AE is above the 45° line ($AE > Y$)**: Inventories fall, and firms increase production, causing GDP to rise toward equilibrium.
*   **If AE is below the 45° line ($AE < Y$)**: Inventories rise, and firms decrease production, causing GDP to fall toward equilibrium.

### Showing a Recession on the 45°-Line Diagram
Equilibrium can occur at a level of GDP below potential GDP. This indicates a recession.
![[Pasted Graphic 20.png]]
*   When the AE line intersects the 45° line at a level of GDP below potential GDP, the economy is in recession.
*   Firms operate below normal capacity, and unemployment is above the natural rate.

## The Multiplier Effect

An initial change in autonomous expenditure leads to a larger, multiplied change in equilibrium real GDP.
*   **Autonomous Expenditure**: Spending that does not depend on the level of GDP (e.g., planned investment, government purchases, net exports, and the autonomous part of consumption).
*   **Multiplier**: The ratio of the increase in equilibrium real GDP to the increase in autonomous expenditure.
*   **Multiplier Effect**: The process by which an increase in autonomous expenditure leads to a larger increase in real GDP. An initial spending increase becomes someone's income, who then spends a portion (the MPC) of it, creating more income, and so on.

![[Pasted Graphic 21.png]]

### The Multiplier Formula
The value of the multiplier depends on the MPC.
$$\text{Multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - MPC}$$
*   The change in equilibrium real GDP due to a change in autonomous expenditure is:
    $$\text{Change in equilibrium real GDP} = \text{Change in autonomous expenditure} \times \text{Multiplier}$$
for invesetment:

$$
\text{Real increase in GDP} = \text{Multiplier} \cdot \text{Planned Increase in Investment}
$$

> [!NOTE] Four Key Points about the Multiplier
> 1.  The effect occurs for both increases and decreases in autonomous expenditure.
> 2.  It makes the economy more sensitive to changes in autonomous spending.
> 3.  The larger the MPC, the larger the value of the multiplier.
> 4.  The simple formula overstates the true value of the multiplier because it ignores real-world factors like taxes, imports, and changes in the price level.

### The Paradox of Thrift
In the short run, an attempt by many households to increase their saving can lead to a fall in aggregate expenditure and push the economy into a recession. The lower incomes during the recession might mean that total saving does not actually increase.

## The Aggregate Demand Curve

The **Aggregate Demand (AD) curve** shows the relationship between the price level and the level of planned aggregate expenditure (or equilibrium real GDP).

### Deriving the AD Curve from the AE Model
A change in the price level shifts the AE curve and leads to a new equilibrium GDP. This relationship traces out the AD curve.
*   **A rising price level** decreases consumption (wealth effect), investment (interest-rate effect), and net exports (international-trade effect). This shifts the AE curve *down*, leading to a *lower* equilibrium GDP.
*   **A falling price level** increases consumption, investment, and net exports. This shifts the AE curve *up*, leading to a *higher* equilibrium GDP.
