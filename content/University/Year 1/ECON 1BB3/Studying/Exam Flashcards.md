What is the Aggregate Demand (AD) and Aggregate Supply (AS) model used for? #flashcard
To explain short-run fluctuations in real GDP and the price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173108-->


Define the Aggregate Demand (AD) Curve. #flashcard
Shows the relationship between the price level and the quantity of real GDP demanded by households, firms, and the government.
<!--ID: 1764547173110-->


Define the Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS) Curve. #flashcard
Shows the relationship between the price level and the quantity of real GDP supplied by firms in the short run.
<!--ID: 1764547173111-->


How are real GDP and the price level determined in the short run? #flashcard
By the intersection of the AD curve and the SRAS curve.
<!--ID: 1764547173112-->


What are the four components of Real GDP (Y) in the aggregate demand equation? #flashcard
$Y = C+I+G+NX$ (Consumption + Investment + Government Purchases + Net Exports)
<!--ID: 1764547173113-->


Why is the Aggregate Demand (AD) curve downward sloping? #flashcard
Due to the wealth effect (on consumption), the interest rate effect (on investment), and the international trade effect (on net exports).
<!--ID: 1764547173114-->


Explain the Wealth Effect on Consumption. #flashcard
When the price level falls, the real value of household wealth (like cash) rises, making households feel wealthier and increasing consumption (C).
<!--ID: 1764547173115-->


Explain the Interest Rate Effect on Investment. #flashcard
When the price level falls, households and firms need less money for transactions, driving down interest rates. Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper, increasing investment (I) and some consumption (C).
<!--ID: 1764547173116-->


Explain the International Trade Effect on Net Exports. #flashcard
If the price level in Canada falls relative to other countries, Canadian exports become cheaper and imports become more expensive, increasing net exports (NX).
<!--ID: 1764547173117-->


What causes a movement along the AD curve? #flashcard
A change in the price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173118-->


What causes the entire AD curve to shift? #flashcard
A change in any variable other than the price level that affects spending.
<!--ID: 1764547173119-->


How does a decrease in interest rates affect the AD curve? #flashcard
Lower interest rates increase consumption (C) and investment (I), shifting the AD curve to the right.
<!--ID: 1764547173120-->


How does an increase in government purchases (G) affect the AD curve? #flashcard
It directly increases aggregate demand, shifting the AD curve to the right.
<!--ID: 1764547173121-->


How does an increase in personal income taxes affect the AD curve? #flashcard
It decreases households' disposable income, which decreases consumption (C), shifting the AD curve to the left.
<!--ID: 1764547173122-->


How does increased optimism by households about future income affect the AD curve? #flashcard
It increases consumption (C), shifting the AD curve to the right.
<!--ID: 1764547173123-->


How does the Canadian dollar appreciating (rising exchange rate) affect the AD curve? #flashcard
It makes Canadian exports more expensive and imports cheaper, decreasing net exports (NX), shifting the AD curve to the left.
<!--ID: 1764547173124-->


What are the two aggregate supply curves? #flashcard
Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS) and Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS).
<!--ID: 1764547173125-->


Define the Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS) Curve. #flashcard
In the long run, the level of real GDP is determined by the supply of inputs (labour force, capital stock) and the available technology.
<!--ID: 1764547173126-->


What is Potential GDP (Full-Employment GDP)? #flashcard
The level of real GDP in the long run, where firms operate at normal capacity and unemployment is at its natural rate.
<!--ID: 1764547173127-->


What is the shape of the LRAS curve and why? #flashcard
It is a vertical line at Potential GDP because potential GDP is not affected by changes in the price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173128-->


What causes the LRAS curve to shift to the right each year? #flashcard
Growth in the labour force, increases in the capital stock, and improvements in technology.
<!--ID: 1764547173129-->


What is the shape of the SRAS curve and why? #flashcard
It is upward sloping because as the price level increases, the quantity of goods and services firms are willing to supply increases due to prices of final goods rising faster than input prices, increasing profitability.
<!--ID: 1764547173130-->


Explain one reason for the upward slope of the SRAS curve related to contracts. #flashcard
Contracts make some wages and prices "sticky." If the price level rises but wages are fixed by a contract, firms become more profitable and increase output.
<!--ID: 1764547173131-->


Explain one reason for the upward slope of the SRAS curve related to wage adjustments. #flashcard
Firms are often slow to adjust non-union worker salaries (e.g., once a year). If the price level rises but wages are slow to adjust, profitability increases, and firms produce more.
<!--ID: 1764547173132-->


Explain one reason for the upward slope of the SRAS curve related to menu costs. #flashcard
Menu costs (costs of changing prices) make some prices sticky. If the price level rises, some firms may not raise prices, increasing their sales and leading them to increase production.
<!--ID: 1764547173133-->


What causes a movement along the SRAS curve? #flashcard
A change in the price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173134-->


What causes the entire SRAS curve to shift? #flashcard
A change in any variable other than the price level that affects the willingness of firms to supply goods.
<!--ID: 1764547173135-->


How do increases in the labour force and capital stock affect the SRAS curve? #flashcard
Firms can supply more output at every price level, shifting the SRAS curve to the right.
<!--ID: 1764547173136-->


How does positive technological change affect the SRAS curve? #flashcard
It increases productivity, reduces firms' costs, and allows them to produce more at every price level, shifting the SRAS curve to the right.
<!--ID: 1764547173137-->


How do expected increases in the future price level affect the SRAS curve? #flashcard
Workers and firms adjust wages and prices upward, increasing firms' costs and shifting the SRAS curve to the left.
<!--ID: 1764547173138-->


What is a Supply Shock and how does an unexpected increase in the price of oil affect the SRAS curve? #flashcard
A supply shock is an unexpected event that shifts the SRAS curve. An unexpected increase in the price of oil raises production costs, shifting the SRAS curve to the left.
<!--ID: 1764547173139-->


When does Long-Run Macroeconomic Equilibrium occur? #flashcard
Where the AD and SRAS curves intersect at a point on the LRAS curve, meaning the economy is at potential GDP.
<!--ID: 1764547173140-->


Describe the short-run effect of a recession caused by a decline in AD. #flashcard
AD shifts left, leading to a new short-run equilibrium with real GDP below potential GDP and a falling price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173141-->


Describe the long-run adjustment mechanism after a recession caused by a decline in AD. #flashcard
Unemployment makes workers accept lower wages and firms accept lower prices, reducing costs and shifting SRAS to the right, returning the economy to potential GDP at a permanently lower price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173142-->


Describe the short-run effect of an expansion caused by an increase in AD. #flashcard
AD shifts right, leading to a new short-run equilibrium with real GDP above potential GDP and a rising price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173143-->


Describe the long-run adjustment mechanism after an expansion caused by an increase in AD. #flashcard
Operating above normal capacity leads to low unemployment and higher wages, increasing costs and shifting SRAS to the left, returning the economy to potential GDP at a permanently higher price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173144-->


Describe the short-run effect of a negative supply shock (e.g., rise in oil prices). #flashcard
SRAS shifts left, leading to a new short-run equilibrium with lower real GDP (recession) and a higher price level.
<!--ID: 1764547173145-->


What is stagflation? #flashcard
The unpleasant combination of recession (stagnation) and inflation, typically caused by a negative supply shock.
<!--ID: 1764547173146-->


Describe the long-run adjustment mechanism after a negative supply shock. #flashcard
The recession and high unemployment eventually cause workers to accept lower wages and firms to accept lower prices, shifting SRAS back to the right and returning the economy to the original equilibrium (Point A) at potential GDP.
<!--ID: 1764547173147-->


In the dynamic AD-AS model, what is the usual cause of inflation? #flashcard
Inflation occurs when the AD curve shifts to the right by *more* than the LRAS (and SRAS) curves shift, meaning total spending grows faster than total production.
<!--ID: 1764547173148-->


What is the target inflation rate in Canada? #flashcard
2%
<!--ID: 1764547173149-->


Why is negative inflation (deflation) undesirable? #flashcard
If prices are expected to fall, consumers delay purchases, leading to decreased demand and causing a recession.
<!--ID: 1764547173150-->


Why do prices generally not come down even with low inflation? #flashcard
Because inflation is typically > 0, meaning prices are still rising, just at a slower rate. People often confuse inflation (rate of change) with the price level itself.
<!--ID: 1764547173151-->


What is the relationship between low unemployment and wages in an expansion? #flashcard
Low unemployment leads to upward pressure on nominal wages (W).
<!--ID: 1764547173152-->


What do firms and workers care about regarding wages? #flashcard
They care about buying power, or the real wage ($W/P$).
<!--ID: 1764547173153-->


What happens to firms' production when the price of an input (like wages) goes up? #flashcard
Firms cut production.
<!--ID: 1764547173154-->


What is the effect of high unemployment on wages in a recession? #flashcard
High unemployment leads to downward pressure on wages.
<!--ID: 1764547173155-->


Why are wages more flexible in the upward direction than the downward direction? #flashcard
Workers are generally resistant to nominal wage cuts ("I don't want to be paid less").
<!--ID: 1764547173156-->


What happens to labor costs for firms if the real wage ($W/P$) goes down? #flashcard
Labor becomes inexpensive, encouraging firms to hire more or produce more.
<!--ID: 1764547173157-->


What causes the AD curve to shift left? #flashcard
A decrease in any component of GDP (Y), such as consumption, investment, government purchases, or net exports.
<!--ID: 1764547173158-->


How does an increase in taxes affect household spending and the AD curve? #flashcard
When taxes go up, disposable incomes go down, households spend less, shifting the AD curve left.
<!--ID: 1764547173159-->


If two variables are changing the AD curve in opposite directions, what can be said about the overall change? #flashcard
The change is ambiguous; the net effect depends on the magnitude of each opposing shift.
<!--ID: 1764547173160-->


When an economy is in an expansion (equilibrium GDP > potential GDP), what happens as it moves from short-run to long-run equilibrium? #flashcard
The price level increases, and real GDP decreases back to potential GDP.
<!--ID: 1764547173161-->


How do automatic stabilizers affect the business cycle? #flashcard
They lessen the severity of swings around the long-run equilibrium (real GDP), making peaks and troughs shallower.
<!--ID: 1764547173162-->

What does M1+ include? #flashcard
Currency (paper money and coins held by households and firms, not banks) and the value of all chequable deposits at chartered banks, TMLs, and CUCPs.

Everything in M1+ plus all non-chequable deposits at chartered banks, TMLs, and CUCPs.

Currency, personal deposits at chartered banks, non-personal demand and notice deposits at chartered banks, and fixed-term deposits.

What does M2++ include? #flashcard
Everything in M2+ plus other retail instruments and non-money market mutual funds.
<!--ID: 1764547173183-->


What does M3 include? #flashcard
Everything in M2 plus non-personal term deposits at chartered banks and foreign currency deposits of residents at chartered banks.
<!--ID: 1764547173184-->


What are the two key points about the money supply? #flashcard
It consists of *both* currency and chequing/non-chequing account deposits, and banks play an important role in how the money supply changes.

How does a bank making a loan (by increasing the borrower's chequing account) affect the money supply? #flashcard

Given $M = C + D$, $C + R = 15$, $D = 2C$, and $D = 3R$, calculate the money supply (M). #flashcard
From $D = 2C$ and $D = 3R$, we get $2C = 3R \implies C = (3/2)R$. Substitute into $C + R = 15$: $(3/2)R + R = 15 \implies (5/2)R = 15 \implies 5R = 30 \implies R = 6$. Then $C = 15 - R = 15 - 6 = 9$. And $D = 2C = 2 \times 9 = 18$. So $M = C + D = 9 + 18 = 27$.

If the growth rate of money supply is 10%, the growth rate of velocity is 0%, and the growth rate of real GDP is 2%, what is the inflation rate according to the quantity theory of money? #flashcard
