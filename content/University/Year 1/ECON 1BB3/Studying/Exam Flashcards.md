Given $M = C + D$, $C + R = 15$, $D = 2C$, and $D = 3R$, calculate the money supply (M).
From $D = 2C$ and $D = 3R$, we get $2C = 3R \implies C = (3/2)R$. Substitute into $C + R = 15$: $(3/2)R + R = 15 \implies (5/2)R = 15 \implies 5R = 30 \implies R = 6$. Then $C = 15 - R = 15 - 6 = 9$. And $D = 2C = 2 \times 9 = 18$. So $M = C + D = 9 + 18 = 27$.

#flashcard
What is a monetary growth rule, and who advocated for it?
Increasing the money supply at a constant rate (e.g., equal to the long-run annual growth rate of real GDP), advocated by monetarists like Milton Friedman.

#flashcard
Why doesn't the Bank of Canada target both the money supply and the interest rate simultaneously?
Because it controls the money *supply* but not money *demand*, and only combinations representing equilibrium in the money market are attainable. It must choose one target.

#flashcard
What is the Taylor Rule?
A rule that explains how central banks set the overnight interest rate target, starting with an equilibrium real overnight interest rate and adjusting for inflation and output gaps.

#flashcard
What is the formula for the Taylor Rule?
$$\text{Overnight interest target rate} = \text{Inflation rate} + \text{Equilibrium real overnight interest rate} + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Inflation gap}) + \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Output gap})$$

#flashcard
Define inflation gap in the Taylor Rule.
Current inflation - target inflation rate.

#flashcard
Define output gap in the Taylor Rule.
Percentage difference between real GDP and potential GDP.

#flashcard
What is the Taylor principle?
When the inflation rate increases by one percentage point, the central bank increases the overnight interest rate by *more than one percentage point* (e.g., 1.5 percentage points) so that the real interest rate also rises, ensuring monetary policy is stabilizing.

#flashcard
What does a negative interest rate mean?
The borrower pays the lender back *less* than the amount borrowed. This is very rare for nominal interest rates.

#flashcard
What is the zero lower bound problem?
The situation where the Taylor rule might suggest a negative nominal interest rate, but central banks cannot effectively push nominal rates below zero.

#flashcard
Define inflation targeting.
A central bank publicly sets an explicit target for the inflation rate over time, and its performance is judged on hitting this target.

#flashcard
What is average-inflation targeting, adopted by the Fed in 2020?
Aiming for inflation that averages 2% over time, meaning it will seek inflation moderately above 2% after periods of below-target inflation.

#flashcard
What is nominal GDP targeting?
Setting a target for nominal GDP growth (e.g., 5% if real GDP growth is 3% and inflation target is 2%).

#flashcard
What is a housing "bubble"?
When housing prices nearly double while rents rise much less, driven by investors overestimating true asset value and expecting to profit by selling at even higher prices.

#flashcard
What are subprime loans?
Loans to borrowers with flawed credit histories, which led to heavy losses for lenders during the 2007-2009 financial crisis.

#flashcard
What is a credit crunch?
Tightened lending requirements, often occurring during financial crises, making it harder for firms and households to borrow.

#flashcard
What role did Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac play in the US mortgage market?
These government-sponsored enterprises bought mortgages from banks and sold mortgage-backed securities to investors, creating a secondary market for mortgages.

#flashcard
What is the moral hazard problem in the context of financial crises?
The risk that firms will take riskier investments if they expect government bailouts in case of failure.

#flashcard
What was the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)?
A program where the US Treasury provided funds to commercial banks in exchange for stock, taking partial ownership, to stabilize the financial system during the 2008 crisis.

What was the Bank of Canada's primary response to the Covid-19 recession in March 2020 regarding its policy rate?
It rapidly cut its policy rate to 0.25%, its effective lower bound.

What are standing liquidity facilities and term repo operations, and how were they enhanced during the Covid-19 recession?
They are tools for the Bank of Canada to lend to financial institutions. They were enhanced by expanding eligible institutions, widening accepted collateral, and lengthening lending terms to manage liquidity.

Define fiscal policy.
Changes in federal taxes and spending that are intended to achieve macroeconomic policy goals.

#flashcard
Distinguish between fiscal policy and monetary policy.
Fiscal policy involves federal taxes and spending (managed by the government), while monetary policy involves managing the money supply and interest rates (managed by the central bank).

#flashcard
Define automatic stabilizers.
Government spending and taxes that automatically increase or decrease along with the business cycle without any new action by the government.

#flashcard
Give an example of an automatic stabilizer during a recession.
Government spending on Employment Insurance automatically increases, and tax revenue automatically falls, cushioning the economic downturn.

#flashcard
Define discretionary fiscal policy.
When the government takes deliberate actions to change spending or taxes (e.g., passing a new bill to cut taxes).

#flashcard
What is the difference between government purchases and government expenditures?
Government purchases are spending where the government receives a good or service in return (e.g., buying fighter jets). Government expenditures include purchases *plus* transfer payments (e.g., Old Age Security).

#flashcard
What are the three primary sources of federal government revenue in Canada?
Personal income taxes (largest), corporate income taxes (second largest), and GST (third largest).

#flashcard
Define expansionary fiscal policy.
Increasing government purchases or decreasing taxes.

#flashcard
What is the goal of expansionary fiscal policy?
To increase aggregate demand and combat a recession.

#flashcard
How does increasing government purchases affect aggregate demand?
It directly increases aggregate demand (AD).

#flashcard
How does decreasing taxes affect aggregate demand?
It increases households' disposable income, which indirectly increases consumption spending (C) and thus aggregate demand (AD).

#flashcard
What are the results of expansionary fiscal policy on real GDP and the price level?
Real GDP and the price level rise.

#flashcard
Define contractionary fiscal policy.
Decreasing government purchases or increasing taxes.

#flashcard
What is the goal of contractionary fiscal policy?
To decrease aggregate demand and combat rising inflation.

#flashcard
How does increasing taxes affect aggregate demand in a contractionary policy?
It reduces disposable income, which decreases consumption spending (C) and thus aggregate demand (AD).

#flashcard
What are the results of contractionary fiscal policy on real GDP and the price level?
Real GDP and the price level fall (or rise more slowly than they would have otherwise).

#flashcard
In the dynamic AD-AS model, when is expansionary fiscal policy used?
When AD is not increasing enough to keep up with the growth in LRAS, to shift AD further right and reach the new long-run equilibrium.

#flashcard
In the dynamic AD-AS model, when is contractionary fiscal policy used?
When AD is increasing too much, pushing the economy beyond potential GDP and causing high inflation, to reduce the shift in AD and bring the economy back to long-run equilibrium.

#flashcard
Define the multiplier effect.
The series of induced increases in consumption spending that results from an initial increase in autonomous expenditures.

#flashcard
What is autonomous spending?
Initial government spending that does not depend on the level of real GDP.

#flashcard
What is induced spending?
Subsequent increases in consumption spending that result from the increased income generated by autonomous spending.

#flashcard
What is the formula for the Government Purchases Multiplier?
$$ \text{Government purchases multiplier} = \frac{\text{Change in equilibrium real GDP}}{\text{Change in government purchases}} $$

#flashcard
What is the formula for the Tax Multiplier?
$$ \text{Tax multiplier} = \frac{\text{Change in equilibrium real GDP}}{\text{Change in taxes}} $$

#flashcard
Why is the tax multiplier always a negative number?
Because a tax *cut* increases GDP, and a tax *hike* decreases GDP.

#flashcard
Why is the absolute value of the tax multiplier smaller than the government purchases multiplier?
Because households will save a portion of a tax cut, so not all of it gets spent in the first round.

#flashcard
How does an upward-sloping SRAS curve affect the actual increase in real GDP from a multiplier effect?
The actual increase in real GDP will be smaller than what the simple multiplier would predict because an increase in AD also leads to a higher price level, which reduces the total quantity of goods demanded.

#flashcard
What are the two main timing lags that make discretionary fiscal policy difficult?
Legislative lag (time to pass legislation) and implementation lag (time to put the policy into effect).

#flashcard
What is crowding out?
A decline in private expenditures (consumption, investment, net exports) as a result of an increase in government purchases.

#flashcard
Describe short-run crowding out.
Increased government spending raises real GDP and money demand, which increases interest rates. Higher interest rates then reduce private investment, consumption of durables, and net exports (due to currency appreciation), making fiscal policy less effective.

#flashcard
What is complete crowding out in the long run?
A permanent increase in government spending results in private spending falling by the same amount that government spending increased.

#flashcard
Define budget deficit.
When the federal government’s expenditures are greater than its tax revenue.

#flashcard
Define budget surplus.
When the federal government’s expenditures are less than its tax revenue.

#flashcard
Define federal government debt.
The total value of government bonds outstanding, equal to the sum of all past budget deficits minus any budget surpluses.

#flashcard
How does the budget automatically move toward a deficit during a recession?
Tax revenues decrease (due to falling wages/profits) and government spending on transfer payments (like EI) increases.

#flashcard
Define cyclically adjusted budget deficit or surplus.
The deficit or surplus in the federal government’s budget if the economy were at potential GDP, used to separate automatic stabilizer effects from discretionary fiscal policy.

#flashcard
Why do few economists believe the budget should be balanced every single year?
Trying to balance the budget during a recession would require raising taxes or cutting spending, which would make the recession worse.

#flashcard
What are some potential problems with a large government debt?
Interest payments can become a significant portion of government spending, forcing tax hikes or cuts to other programs, and a rising debt-to-GDP ratio can crowd out private investment, slowing long-term economic growth.

#flashcard
What is supply-side economics?
Fiscal policies designed to affect aggregate supply rather than aggregate demand, with the goal of promoting long-run economic growth.

#flashcard
Define tax wedge.
The difference between the pre-tax and post-tax return to an economic activity.

#flashcard
How do individual income tax cuts (reducing marginal tax rates) affect the economy in the long run?
They increase the return to working and saving, which can increase the supply of labour and loanable funds, shifting LRAS right.

#flashcard
How do corporate income tax cuts affect the economy in the long run?
They increase the after-tax return on investment, encouraging firms to invest more in capital and potentially increasing the pace of technological change, shifting LRAS right.

What is the main debate among economists regarding supply-side effects of fiscal policy?
Not *if* they exist, but *how large* they are, with skeptics arguing the effects on aggregate supply are small and tax cuts primarily affect aggregate demand.

$\frac{-MPC}{1-MPC}$ (where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume).

What is the balanced budget multiplier?
If government spending ($\Delta G$) and taxes ($\Delta T$) both increase by the same amount, the AD curve will shift by that amount, with a multiplier of 1.

$\frac{1}{1-MPC} + \frac{-MPC}{1-MPC} = \frac{1-MPC}{1-MPC} = 1$
